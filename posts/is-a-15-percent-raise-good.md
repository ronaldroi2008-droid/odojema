# Is a 15% Raise Good?

Yes, a 15% raise is generally considered a very good salary increase. The short answer to "is a 15 percent salary increase good" is almost always yes: it is significantly higher than many routine annual raises and is often associated with promotions, expanded responsibilities, market adjustments, or exceptional performance. While the impact depends on your industry, salary level, and career goals, a 15% raise is commonly viewed as a substantial increase.

Here's how it stacks up at a glance:

| Raise Percentage | Common Interpretation |
|---|---|
| 2%–3% | Typical annual raise |
| 4%–5% | Strong raise |
| 6%–9% | Very strong raise |
| 10%–14% | Large raise |
| 15%+ | Often promotion-level raise |

As that table makes clear, a 15% raise doesn't just edge past the typical annual bump — it sits in a category most employees rarely see outside of a promotion, a market correction, or a genuinely exceptional review cycle. The rest of this guide breaks down exactly why that is, what a 15% raise looks like in real dollars, and how to judge whether your own situation makes that number as good as it sounds on paper.

## How Good Is a 15% Raise?

A 15% raise is well above what many employees receive during a normal performance review. Most companies budget standard merit increases somewhere in the low single digits, which means a raise in the double digits already puts you ahead of the typical curve before you even look at the dollar amount.

Here's a simple example to make it concrete:

**Current Salary:** $50,000

**15% Raise:**
$50,000 × 15% = **$7,500**

**New Salary:** $57,500

A raise of this size can have a meaningful impact on monthly income and future earnings. $7,500 a year isn't a rounding error — it's real, usable money that can go toward debt payoff, savings, or simply a more comfortable monthly budget. And because raises typically compound on top of each other over the course of a career (more on that below), the long-term value of a 15% increase is often larger than the first-year number suggests.

## How Does a 15% Raise Compare to Average Raises?

To understand why a 15% raise stands out, it helps to see where it falls relative to the full range of raises employees typically receive:

| Raise Type | Typical Range |
|---|---|
| Annual Raise | 2%–4% |
| Strong Raise | 4%–6% |
| Exceptional Raise | 7%–10% |
| Promotion Raise | 10%–15%+ |
| Market Adjustment | Varies |

A 15% raise sits near the top end of nearly every one of these ranges. It's roughly four to seven times larger than a routine annual raise, comfortably above what most companies would even label an "exceptional" performance-based increase, and right at the upper boundary of what's typically associated with a promotion. In practice, this means that if you've received a 15% raise without changing roles or taking on new responsibilities, something unusual — and favorable — is likely going on: a retention effort, a market correction, or recognition of performance well above the norm.

## How Much Money Is a 15% Raise?

Because a raise is a percentage, the actual dollar impact depends heavily on your starting salary. Here's how a 15% raise plays out at a few common salary levels:

**Example 1**
Salary: $40,000
15% Raise = $6,000
New Salary = $46,000

**Example 2**
Salary: $60,000
15% Raise = $9,000
New Salary = $69,000

**Example 3**
Salary: $80,000
15% Raise = $12,000
New Salary = $92,000

Notice how the dollar value scales directly with salary — the same 15% is worth $6,000 at one income level and $12,000 at another. This is an important nuance to keep in mind when comparing raises across different jobs, industries, or coworkers: a 15% raise is always proportionally significant, but its absolute dollar value will look very different depending on where you're starting from.

## Is a 15% Raise Good for a Promotion?

In many organizations, yes. A promotion often includes:

- Higher responsibilities
- Leadership duties
- New skills requirements
- Larger decision-making authority

Because of this, promotion raises frequently exceed standard annual raises. Companies generally recognize that a new title alone isn't enough compensation for a meaningfully expanded role — the pay needs to reflect the added scope, accountability, and (often) the new skills the position demands. A 15% increase tied to a promotion is a reasonable, commonly cited benchmark, and in many cases it's treated as being on the healthier end of what a promotion typically pays.

That said, "typical" doesn't mean "guaranteed." Promotion raises can vary widely depending on company size, industry, budget constraints, and how significant the actual change in responsibility is. A lateral move to a slightly more senior title might come with a smaller bump, while a jump into true leadership or management responsibility might justify something well above 15%. ## Is a 15% Raise Better Than Changing Jobs?

This is one of the most common questions that comes up once someone is offered a 15% raise — is it better to stay and take it, or would switching jobs actually pay off more? There's no universal answer, but weighing the tradeoffs clearly can help.

**Staying**

Benefits:
- Familiar environment
- Existing benefits
- Career progression

Staying with your current employer means keeping the relationships, institutional knowledge, and established benefits (retirement contributions, accrued leave, healthcare continuity) that often get reset or renegotiated when you change jobs. A 15% raise while staying put also usually comes with continuity in your career trajectory — you're building on an existing track record rather than starting over.

**Switching Jobs**

Benefits:
- Potentially larger salary increases
- New opportunities
- Faster career growth

Changing employers, on the other hand, is frequently cited as one of the fastest ways to secure a larger salary jump than internal raises typically allow, since a new employer is often competing for your skills rather than managing an existing budget line for your current role. Switching can also open doors to new responsibilities, industries, or career paths that may not have been available internally.

Compensation is only one factor. A 15% raise might be the better financial outcome in the short term, but a new role might offer better long-term growth, a healthier work environment, or skills that pay off down the line. The right choice ultimately depends on how you weigh stability and familiarity against growth and opportunity.

## What Does a 15% Raise Mean Monthly?

Annual raise figures can feel abstract, so it often helps to break the number down into what actually shows up in your paycheck each month.

**Example:**
$50,000 Salary
15% Raise = $7,500

Monthly Increase:
$7,500 ÷ 12 ≈ **$625 per month**

Show why percentage raises can have a noticeable effect on take-home pay: an extra $625 a month is enough to meaningfully move the needle on a car payment, a chunk of rent, or a solid monthly savings contribution. Seeing the raise in monthly terms — rather than just as an abstract annual percentage — often makes it clearer just how substantial a 15% increase really is in everyday terms.

## What Is a 15% Raise After Taxes?

The examples above show the raise before taxes, but your actual take-home increase will be smaller once payroll withholding is factored in. Using the same $50,000 salary example:

**Raise:** $7,500

**Estimated Taxes:** 20%–30%

**Net Increase:** about $5,250–$6,000

The exact percentage withheld depends on your tax bracket, filing status, state and local taxes, and any pre-tax deductions (like retirement contributions or health insurance) that change alongside your new salary. A raise that pushes part of your income into a higher bracket won't have your entire salary taxed at that higher rate — only the portion above the bracket threshold — but it's still worth budgeting around the net figure rather than the gross raise amount when planning how the extra income will actually affect your monthly budget.

## How a 15% Raise Compounds Over Time

One of the most overlooked aspects of a raise is what happens when it's not just a one-time event. Raises build on top of each other over a career, which means each subsequent increase is calculated against an already-higher base.

**Starting Salary:** $50,000

**After 15% Raise:** $57,500

**After Another 15% Raise:** $66,125

**After Third 15% Raise:** $76,044

This is the effect of compounding: because each raise is applied to the new, higher salary rather than the original figure, the dollar value of each subsequent 15% increase grows larger even though the percentage stays the same. Over a full career, consistently strong raises — even if they don't happen every single year — can add up to dramatically higher earnings than a career of only small, routine increases. This is part of why negotiating well early in your career, or securing a strong promotion raise sooner rather than later, tends to have an outsized effect on lifetime earnings.

## When Might a 15% Raise Not Feel Large?

Despite being well above average on paper, there are real situations where a 15% raise might not feel as impactful as the number suggests:

- **High inflation periods** — if the cost of living has risen sharply, a chunk of your raise may simply be absorbing higher prices rather than translating into genuinely more purchasing power.
- **Significant workload increases** — if the raise comes alongside a much heavier workload or expanded hours, the effective "value per hour worked" may not feel proportionally better.
- **Below-market starting salary** — if your salary was unusually low to begin with, even a 15% increase might only bring you closer to a fair market rate rather than meaningfully above it.
- **Increased living costs** — a move to a higher cost-of-living area, new family expenses, or other rising personal costs can offset the practical impact of a raise that would otherwise feel generous.

Context matters. A 15% raise is a strong number in isolation, but how "good" it actually feels depends heavily on what else is happening in your financial life and how it compares to your specific market and circumstances.

## Factors That Influence Whether a 15% Raise Is Good

Several variables shape whether a 15% raise should be considered excellent, solid, or just adequate for your specific situation:

- **Industry** — some industries routinely offer larger raises and bonuses than others, which shifts what counts as impressive.
- **Location** — cost of living and regional pay scales affect how far a given raise actually goes.
- **Experience Level** — early-career raises often run higher in percentage terms, while senior employees may see smaller percentage increases on a much larger base salary.
- **Market Demand** — in-demand skills or roles tend to command larger raises, especially when employers are trying to prevent you from leaving.
- **Company Performance** — a company having a strong year is generally in a better position to offer larger raises than one that's cutting costs.
- **Job Responsibilities** — a raise tied to a genuine increase in scope or accountability carries different weight than the same percentage given without any change in role.

Weighing these factors against the flat 15% figure gives a much more accurate picture of whether your specific raise is genuinely strong, or simply average dressed up as a big number.

## How to Calculate a 15% Raise

If you want to calculate the exact dollar value of a 15% raise for your own salary, the formula is straightforward:

**Formula:**
Raise Amount = Current Salary × 0.15

**Example:**
$70,000 × 0.15 = **$10,500**

**New Salary:**
$70,000 + $10,500 = **$80,500**

This same formula works for any salary figure — simply multiply your current salary by 0.15 to find the raise amount, then add that number to your original salary to get your new total.
