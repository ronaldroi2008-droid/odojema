*Last updated: October 15, 2026.*

For a regular yearly raise, **3% to 5% is a good raise** in most U.S. jobs. Anything above 5% is strong, and anything below about 3% may not keep up with rising prices. Raises of 10% or more usually come with a promotion, a new role, or a job change.

The right number for you depends on three things: what other employers are paying, how fast prices are rising, and why you are getting the raise in the first place. This guide covers all three, shows what each raise percentage means in real dollars (before and after tax), and gives you a plan if your raise is lower than you hoped. You can test your own numbers at any point with our [raise calculator](https://odojema.com/raise-calculator).

**Quick answers**

- **Average raise in 2026:** a little over 3% (about 3.1% merit, about 3.4% total)
- **Good yearly raise:** 3% to 5%
- **Great yearly raise:** 5% or more
- **Typical promotion raise:** about 8% to 10%
- **Typical raise when changing jobs:** 10% to 20% or more

## What is the average raise in 2026?

Employer surveys give a consistent picture of what the typical worker receives:

| Measure | Typical figure |
|---|---|
| Average merit raise paid in 2026 | about 3.1% |
| Average total raise paid in 2026 (merit, promotions, cost-of-living, other) | about 3.4% |
| Projected average merit raise for 2027 | about 3.2% |
| Projected average total raise for 2027 | about 3.5% |
| Average raise for a one-level promotion | about 8% to 9% |

These figures come from employer compensation surveys by Mercer and WTW, published between late 2025 and mid-2026. They are averages across many employers and include some workers who received no raise, so your own number can sit above or below them.

In plain terms, **the average U.S. raise is a little over 3%**. If you received 3% to 4%, you are close to the middle of the pack. If you received 5% or more, you are above average.

### Merit raise vs. total raise

You will see two numbers in most reports, and they mean different things:

- **Merit raise:** the yearly increase tied to your performance review. This is the number most people mean when they ask "what raise should I get?"
- **Total raise:** everything that raised pay budgets during the year, including merit raises, promotions, cost-of-living adjustments, and market corrections. It is usually a few tenths of a point higher than the merit figure.

If your manager says your company's budget is "3%," that is usually the merit pool that gets divided among employees, with stronger performers getting more and weaker performers getting less.

### Average raise by industry

Raises are surprisingly similar across industries, but there are small differences. In Mercer's 2026 survey, no industry paid an average merit raise above 3.2%. Here is how the picture looked for total increases:

| Industry group | Average total raise (2026) |
|---|---|
| High tech | about 3.6% |
| Health care services | about 3.3% |
| Retail, wholesale, and transportation equipment | about 3.1% |
| Chemicals and other manufacturing | about 2.9% |

The gap between the highest and lowest group is less than one percentage point. That means switching industries rarely changes your yearly raise much. What changes your pay more is your role, your skills, and whether you change employers.

## Is the raise bigger than inflation?

A raise only improves your life if it is bigger than the rise in prices. If your pay goes up 3% and prices go up 3%, you can buy exactly what you could before. If prices rise faster than your raise, your buying power falls even though your paycheck is bigger.

Check the current inflation rate on the [Bureau of Labor Statistics CPI page](https://www.bls.gov/cpi/) and compare it with your raise:

- **Raise above inflation:** you are getting ahead.
- **Raise equal to inflation:** you are staying even.
- **Raise below inflation:** you are taking a real pay cut.

Here is a simple example. Say you earn $60,000 and get a 3% raise ($1,800), but prices rise 4% over the same year. To keep your buying power, you would have needed $2,400. Your raise is real money, but after inflation it works out to a pay cut of roughly 1%. The reverse also applies: in a year with 2% inflation, a 3% raise gives you a real gain of about 1%.

This is why a 2% raise can feel like nothing in a year with higher inflation, and a 3% raise can feel generous in a year with low inflation.

### How much of the raise do you actually keep?

A raise is taxed like the rest of your pay, so your take-home increase is smaller than the headline number. For a single filer earning around $60,000 in 2026, the extra income is taxed at the 12% federal rate (the 12% bracket runs from $12,400 up to $50,400 of taxable income after the $16,100 standard deduction, so $60,000 gross lands inside it), plus 7.65% for Social Security and Medicare. Together that is about 19.65% before any state income tax.

| Raise on $60,000 | Gross increase | Approximate take-home increase | Per month | Per biweekly paycheck |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 3% | $1,800 | about $1,446 | about $121 | about $56 |
| 5% | $3,000 | about $2,410 | about $201 | about $93 |
| 10% | $6,000 | about $4,821 | about $402 | about $185 |

State and local income taxes would lower these numbers further. Your own situation may differ based on filing status, deductions, and benefit contributions, so treat this as a rough guide. The takeaway is that a raise of about 3% adds roughly $120 a month to your take-home pay at this salary, which is why a small percentage can feel invisible in your paycheck.

## Is a 2%, 3%, 4%, 5%, 7.5%, 10%, or 15% raise good?

Here is a more detailed verdict for each size of raise, for a regular yearly review:

### 1% to 2%: below average

This is often less than inflation, so it can mean a real pay cut. A 2% raise on $60,000 is $1,200 a year, or about $100 a month before tax. It is not unusual in a tight year, but it is reasonable to ask why, and what it would take to get more next cycle. If you also took on more work, a 2% raise is a good reason to ask for a pay review.

### 3%: about average

This matches what most employers paid in 2026. It is a fine result in a low-inflation year, but it is not generous. If your performance reviews are strong and you got 3%, ask how your rating compares with others on your team. See [is a 3% raise good](https://odojema.com/blog/is-a-3-percent-raise-good) for a deeper breakdown.

### 4%: solid

Slightly above the average and a fair result for good performance. A 4% raise on $60,000 is $2,400 a year, and it compounds well over time (see the table below).

### 5%: a good raise

Clearly above average for an annual raise, and often reserved for strong performers. On $60,000, it adds $3,000 a year. If you get 5% in a year when the company average is closer to 3%, your manager is signaling that you are valued. See [is a 5% raise good](https://odojema.com/blog/is-a-5-percent-raise-good).

### 7.5%: very good

Rare for a yearly merit raise. Most common for top performers, for people whose pay was below market, or when a raise is combined with a larger change in duties. If you get 7.5% without a title change, it is worth asking whether it reflects a market adjustment, because that tells you how your employer sees your pay range.

### 10%: excellent for a yearly raise, normal for a promotion

If you get 10% outside a promotion, it likely corrects underpay or rewards exceptional results. If it comes with a promotion, it is right in the normal range. A 10% raise on $60,000 adds $6,000 a year. See [is a 10% raise good](https://odojema.com/blog/is-a-10-percent-raise-good).

### 15% or more: a large raise

Usually tied to a major promotion, a big change in responsibilities, a market adjustment, or a new job. If you are offered this in your current job without a change in role, it is worth asking what changed, and whether it is permanent or a one-time adjustment.

## What is considered a large raise?

For a yearly raise at the same job, anything above about **7% to 8%** counts as a large raise, since the average is a little over 3%. For a promotion, a raise of **10% to 15%** is common and not unusual. When you switch employers, many people see 10% to 20% or more, because a new employer sets pay by market rate, not by a small annual budget.

Large raises are not always positive signals on their own. A 15% raise to take on a second person's workload may be worth less than a 5% raise for the same job you already do. Always compare the raise with the change in your responsibilities.

## What counts as a fair raise?

A fair raise is one that matches three things:

1. **The market.** Compare your pay with what employers pay for the same role in your area. If your pay is below market, a fair raise should close the gap, and a cost-of-living increase alone will not.
2. **Your performance.** Strong results usually deserve more than the company average. Weak results usually get less.
3. **Inflation.** A fair raise should at least keep your buying power, unless the company is in real financial trouble.

If you are well below market, asking for a market adjustment can get you a bigger result than waiting for a regular raise cycle. Employers know this: surveys show that off-cycle pay adjustments have become a common tool, used by about two-thirds of employers in 2025, up from about half in 2023.

## Good raise by situation

The same percentage means different things in different situations:

| Situation | Typical raise | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Annual merit raise | 3% to 4% | Average is a little over 3% |
| Cost-of-living adjustment | 2% to 3% | Meant to match inflation, not reward performance |
| Strong performer | 4% to 6% | Above average for the company |
| Promotion | 8% to 10%, often more | One-level promotions average about 8% to 9% |
| Market adjustment | 5% to 15% | When pay is well below market |
| New job offer | 10% to 20% or more | Set by market rate, not annual budget |

### How performance changes your raise

Most companies do not give everyone the same percentage. A common pattern is to start from the company's budget (for example, 3%) and adjust it by performance rating:

- **Top performers:** often 1 to 3 points above the budget (so roughly 4% to 6%)
- **Solid performers:** close to the budget (about 3%)
- **Below expectations:** below the budget, sometimes zero

Mercer's 2026 survey found that only about 4% of employers gave everyone exactly the same raise, so for most workers the result depends on how the review goes. That is why preparing your case before your review matters more than most people expect.

## Raise percentage vs. dollar amount

A percentage raise and a dollar raise can feel very different. A 3% raise on a $40,000 salary is $1,200 a year, and the same 3% on $120,000 is $3,600. If your salary is low, even a good percentage may feel small in dollars, and a negotiated flat amount may be worth more.

## How small raises add up over time

A raise is not a one-time event. It becomes part of your base salary, and every future raise is calculated on top of it. Here is what a $60,000 salary grows to with the same percentage raise each year:

| Years | 2% a year | 3% a year | 4% a year | 5% a year |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | $61,200 | $61,800 | $62,400 | $63,000 |
| 3 | $63,672 | $65,564 | $67,492 | $69,458 |
| 5 | $66,245 | $69,556 | $72,999 | $76,577 |
| 10 | $73,140 | $80,635 | $88,815 | $97,734 |

The gap between a 3% raise and a 5% raise looks small in year one ($1,200), but after ten years it is more than $17,000 a year in salary. This is why arguing for even one extra percentage point at a review is worth the effort. ## How to calculate your raise percentage

The formula is:

```
Raise % = (New salary − Old salary) ÷ Old salary × 100
```

Example: you earned $55,000 and your new salary is $57,200.

```
($57,200 − $55,000) ÷ $55,000 × 100 = 4%
```

To go the other way and find your new salary from a percentage:

```
New salary = Old salary × (1 + Raise % ÷ 100)
$55,000 × 1.04 = $57,200
```

## How to ask for a bigger raise

Whether your review is coming up or already happened, a good request has four parts:

1. **Know your number.** Look up what comparable roles pay in your area, and decide on a specific figure or percentage you want, not a vague "more."
2. **List your results.** Write down what you delivered since your last raise: projects finished, money saved or earned, responsibilities added, and positive feedback.
3. **Pick the right moment.** The best times are during your review, after a major win, or when your duties clearly grew. Budgets are often set months before reviews, so asking early gives you more room. See [when to ask for a raise](https://odojema.com/blog/when-to-ask-for-a-raise).
4. **Ask clearly and calmly.** Say what you want and why, then listen.

A short script you can adapt:

> "Over the past year I took on [responsibility] and delivered [result]. Based on what comparable roles pay, I would like to discuss moving my salary to [number or percentage]. Can we talk about what it would take to get there?"

If you want to make a promotion case instead, see [how to negotiate a promotion raise](https://odojema.com/blog/how-to-negotiate-a-promotion-raise).

## What to do if your raise is lower than you hoped

1. **Ask how the number was set.** Find out if it was a company-wide budget, a performance rating, or a market comparison. The answer tells you what to change.
2. **Ask what would earn more next cycle.** Get specific goals in writing, and a date to check in.
3. **Ask for a mid-year review.** If the budget was the problem, a six-month check-in with a target number gives you a second chance.
4. **Consider other forms of pay.** A signing bonus, extra vacation, flexible hours, a title change, or a training budget can be worth real money when salary is fixed.
5. **Check the market.** If you are well below what similar roles pay elsewhere, that is useful information, even if you stay.

## Common mistakes when judging a raise

- **Looking only at the percentage.** A 5% raise on a low salary can be less money than 3% on a high one. Always check the dollar amount too.
- **Ignoring inflation.** A raise below inflation is a pay cut in real terms.
- **Forgetting taxes.** Take-home pay rises by less than the gross raise.
- **Ignoring new duties.** A raise that comes with a lot more work may be a worse deal than it looks.
- **Comparing with the wrong group.** Compare with similar roles in your area, not the national average or a friend in a different field.
- **Waiting too long to ask.** Many employers set raise budgets before reviews, so early conversations have more impact.

## Frequently asked questions

**What percent raise is good?**
For a yearly raise, 3% to 5% is good. The average in 2026 is a little over 3%, so 4% to 5% is above average.

**What is the average raise in 2026?**
Employer surveys show an average merit raise of about 3.1% and an average total raise of about 3.4%. Projections for 2027 are about 3.2% for merit and 3.5% in total.

**What is a normal yearly raise?**
About 3% to 4%. Raises tend to move within a narrow band across industries and company sizes.

**Is a 3% raise good?**
It is about average. It matches what most employers paid, but it is only a good raise if it is above inflation.

**Is a 5% raise good?**
Yes. It is above the average for an annual raise.

**Is a 10% raise good?**
Yes. A 10% raise is excellent for a yearly review and normal for a promotion.

**What is a good raise for a promotion?**
About 8% to 10% is common, and 10% to 15% is not unusual for a bigger step up.

**How much should I ask for?**
Ask for a number based on market pay for your role, not only on the company average. If you are underpaid, ask for a market adjustment.

**Is a 2% raise bad?**
It is below average and often less than inflation, so it can mean a real pay cut. It is not unusual, but it is reasonable to ask what would earn more next year.

**What is considered a large raise?**
At the same job, more than about 7% to 8%. For a promotion or a new employer, 10% to 20% is common.

**How much of my raise will I keep after taxes?**
For a single filer around $60,000, roughly 80% of a raise in federal terms, before any state income tax, so a $3,000 raise adds about $2,400 to your take-home pay over the year.

**Should I take a raise or a one-time bonus?**
A raise lasts and compounds, while a bonus is paid once. If both are the same dollar amount, the raise is usually worth more over time.
