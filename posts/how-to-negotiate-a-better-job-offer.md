To negotiate a better job offer, research the market salary range, identify your priorities, evaluate the full compensation package, and make a clear counteroffer supported by your qualifications and relevant evidence. Discuss salary and benefits professionally, then review the employer's response before deciding whether to accept.

Receiving a job offer is exciting, but the initial terms may not fully reflect your experience, expectations, or financial needs. Negotiation can cover salary, signing bonuses, benefits, flexibility, and other employment terms. Success isn't guaranteed, and a professional, evidence-based approach tends to work better than making demands. The nine steps below walk through preparing a negotiation, making a request, and responding to whatever the employer says back.

## 1. Understand the Job Offer Before Negotiating

Before you respond to anything, review the offer in full. That means looking at:

- Base salary and pay frequency.
- Bonus or commission structure.
- Health insurance and retirement contributions.
- Paid time off and leave policies.
- Work schedule, remote arrangements, and overtime expectations.
- Start date, job responsibilities, and any other relevant conditions.

It helps to separate guaranteed compensation from conditional or discretionary compensation before you decide what to ask for. If you haven't already worked through the offer in detail, it's worth first learning how to [evaluate a job offer](https://odojema.com/blog/how-to-evaluate-a-job-offer) as a whole, and how to [calculate the true value of a job offer](https://odojema.com/blog/true-value-of-a-job-offer) once you've accounted for benefits and work-related costs. Negotiating from a clear picture of what's actually on the table puts you in a much stronger position than reacting to the salary line alone.

## 2. Research the Appropriate Salary Range

A realistic target comes from comparable positions, location, experience, industry, and the actual responsibilities of the role, not from a guess. Three figures are worth establishing before you say anything to the employer:

- **Target salary:** The amount you hope to achieve.
- **Minimum acceptable salary:** The lowest amount you'd realistically accept.
- **Opening request:** The amount you initially ask for, supported by evidence and leaving reasonable room for discussion.

Resist the temptation to apply a flat percentage increase as a universal rule; the right target depends on your specific situation, not a number that works the same way for every candidate. Good sources for this research include the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, the employer's own job postings for similar roles, and credible industry salary surveys that match your location and experience level.

## 3. Decide What You Want to Negotiate

Rather than negotiating every item on the offer at once, prioritize the terms that matter most to you. Possibilities include:

- Higher base salary.
- A signing bonus.
- Performance bonus or commission terms.
- Additional paid time off.
- Remote or hybrid work arrangements.
- Flexible working hours.
- Professional development support.
- Start date or an agreed-upon compensation review timeline.

Keep in mind that an employer may have different levels of flexibility for each of these. Base salary might be tightly constrained by an internal pay band, while a signing bonus or start date could have much more room to move. If base pay turns out to be the fixed constraint, it's worth comparing [salary and benefits](https://odojema.com/blog/salary-vs-benefits) to see where else value might be available.

## 4. Build a Strong Case for Your Counteroffer

Support your request with concrete evidence rather than a general sense that you deserve more:

- Specialized skills and certifications relevant to the role.
- Relevant experience and measurable past achievements.
- The responsibilities and scope of the position itself.
- Market compensation data for comparable roles.
- The value you can reasonably expect to contribute to the employer.

A confident, collaborative tone tends to land better than personal financial pressure or unsupported claims about competing offers. You're making a case for why the role is worth more to this employer specifically, not asking for a favor.

## 5. How to Make a Salary Counteroffer

A practical sequence for the conversation itself:

1. Thank the employer for the offer.
2. Express genuine interest in the position.
3. State the salary or term you'd like to discuss.
4. Briefly explain the basis for your request.
5. Invite the employer to discuss possible options.

A sample version of this conversation:

> "Thank you for the offer. I'm excited about the opportunity and the responsibilities of this role. Based on the position's scope, my relevant experience, and the market range I've reviewed, would you be open to discussing a base salary of $85,000? I'd be happy to discuss how we might reach an agreement that works for both sides."

The $85,000 figure here is illustrative only, not a recommended salary for every position. Once you have your own researched target, a [raise calculator](https://odojema.com/raise-calculator) can help you work out exactly what a given counteroffer would mean in percentage terms relative to the original offer.

## 6. Use an Email Template to Negotiate a Job Offer

A written counteroffer gives both sides a clear record to refer back to. A reusable template:

> **Subject:** Discussion of Job Offer — [Position Title]
>
> Dear [Hiring Manager's Name],
>
> Thank you for offering me the [Position Title] role at [Company Name]. I appreciate the opportunity and am enthusiastic about the possibility of joining your team.
>
> After reviewing the offer and considering the responsibilities of the position and my relevant experience, I wanted to ask whether there is flexibility to discuss the base salary. I was hoping for a salary of [Target Salary], based on [brief, relevant justification].
>
> I remain very interested in the position and would welcome the opportunity to discuss the compensation package and find an arrangement that works for both sides.
>
> Thank you for your time and consideration.
>
> Best regards,
> [Your Name]

Adapt the wording to your actual offer, use a target you can defend with real reasoning, and remove any justification that isn't accurate for your situation. A template is a starting point, not a script to copy word for word.

## 7. Negotiate Benefits if Salary Is Not Flexible

Sometimes base pay genuinely has no room to move, but that doesn't mean the conversation is over. Other terms worth exploring include a signing bonus, additional paid leave, a more flexible schedule, remote work arrangements, professional development funding, or a written compensation review timeline.

For example, if the employer can't increase the base salary, it's reasonable to ask whether a signing bonus or an earlier salary review might be possible instead. Just keep in mind that a future review is not the same as a guaranteed raise; any agreement along these lines should specify the actual terms in writing, including what criteria would be used and when the review would happen, rather than relying on a verbal understanding.

## 8. What to Do When the Employer Says No

A few different situations can come up once you've made your request:

- The employer rejects the requested salary but offers another benefit instead.
- The employer says the salary range is fixed, full stop.
- The employer makes a revised offer that still falls below your target.

In any of these cases, it helps to ask what flexibility remains, reassess your own minimum acceptable terms given the new information, and make a considered decision rather than reacting immediately. Avoid bluffing about competing offers you don't actually have, issuing ultimatums you're not prepared to follow through on, or assuming the employer will withdraw the offer just because you asked for more. Most employers expect some degree of negotiation and won't penalize a reasonable, professionally framed request.

## 9. Avoid Common Job Offer Negotiation Mistakes

A number of avoidable missteps can undercut an otherwise solid negotiation:

- Asking for an arbitrary salary without any research behind it.
- Negotiating before you've actually understood the entire offer.
- Treating a target or discretionary bonus as guaranteed pay.
- Using an aggressive or entitled tone in your request.
- Inventing competing offers that don't exist.
- Accepting verbal changes to the offer without getting them confirmed in writing.
- Focusing only on salary while ignoring workload, benefits, and other terms that affect your day-to-day experience.
- Assuming every employer has the same flexibility on every term; what worked at a previous job or for a friend's negotiation may not apply here.

## Negotiating Benefits Beyond Base Salary

When you're building your case for the benefits side of the conversation, it helps to understand what you're actually comparing. A [total compensation](https://odojema.com/blog/what-is-total-compensation) view, salary plus retirement contributions, insurance value, and bonuses, often reveals more room to negotiate than base salary alone suggests. An employer that genuinely can't move on the salary line may still have meaningful flexibility on retirement matching eligibility, the timing of your first bonus, or how quickly you become eligible for benefits that normally carry a waiting period.

Framing these requests the same way you'd frame a salary request, specific, evidence-based, and tied to the value you bring, tends to work better than treating benefits negotiation as an afterthought once salary talks have stalled. Ask directly what flexibility exists on each specific benefit rather than a general "can you do better," since a specific question is easier for a hiring manager to answer and act on.

## How Negotiation Differs by Industry and Seniority Level

The approach above holds across most roles, but how much room you actually have to negotiate varies considerably by industry and seniority.

In fields with standardized pay bands, common in government, education, and many large unionized employers, salary negotiation may be genuinely limited or unavailable, with most flexibility existing in start date, signing bonus, or benefits eligibility instead. In industries with more variable compensation structures, such as technology, sales, or specialized consulting, there's often more room to negotiate base salary, equity, and performance-based pay directly.

Seniority matters too. Entry-level candidates typically have less individual leverage, since employers are often comparing many similarly qualified applicants for the same role, though this doesn't mean negotiation is off the table entirely, just that the evidence-based approach matters even more. Senior candidates and those with specialized, hard-to-replace skills generally have more room to negotiate, both because they have more documented achievements to point to and because replacing them is a costlier and slower process for the employer. Knowing where your specific situation falls on this spectrum helps calibrate how assertively to approach the conversation.

## What If You Receive a Competing Offer During Negotiation?

If a competing offer arrives while you're still negotiating, it can be a legitimate point of leverage, but it's worth handling carefully. Mentioning a real competing offer, with honest details about its terms, is different from using a vague or exaggerated reference to "other opportunities" as a pressure tactic; the first is informative, the second tends to come across as a bluff even when it isn't one.

If you do bring up a competing offer, be prepared for the employer to ask for specifics, and only share what you're comfortable having verified. It's also worth being realistic about timing: if you need an answer from one employer before the other has finished its process, it's reasonable to ask each for a short, specific extension rather than guessing at an answer under unnecessary pressure. Most employers would rather give you a few extra days than lose a candidate to a process that moved faster.

## Comparing Salary Negotiation and Benefits Negotiation

It's worth noting that negotiating salary and negotiating benefits aren't quite the same conversation, even though they often happen together. Salary negotiation is usually more constrained by internal pay bands and budget approvals, which can make it the harder lever to move, especially at larger, more standardized employers. Benefits negotiation, by contrast, can sometimes offer more flexibility precisely because individual items, like a signing bonus, an extra week of leave, or a flexible start date, don't always require the same level of approval as a change to base pay.

That doesn't mean benefits are always easier to negotiate; it depends heavily on the specific employer and what's already standardized in their policies. But if salary negotiation stalls, it's often worth shifting the conversation toward benefits rather than treating a fixed salary as the end of the discussion entirely. Before deciding which lever to pull first, it can help to [compare two job offers](https://odojema.com/blog/how-to-compare-job-offers) you're weighing, or use the [Job Offer Comparison Calculator](https://odojema.com/job-offer-comparison-calculator) to see where the real gaps are between what you have and what you want.

## How to Decide Your Negotiation Priorities Before You Start

Before writing any email or having any conversation, it helps to rank what matters most to you personally, since you won't always get everything you ask for. Someone early in their career might prioritize learning opportunities and a reasonable starting salary over a signing bonus, while someone with significant family financial obligations might prioritize guaranteed cash compensation over flexible scheduling.

Write down your top two or three priorities before you negotiate, and be honest with yourself about which ones you'd actually walk away over versus which ones are nice to have. This isn't just useful for your own clarity; it also helps you respond quickly and confidently if the employer counters with "we can't move on salary, but we can offer X instead," rather than needing time to figure out on the spot whether X is actually a reasonable trade.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is it okay to negotiate a job offer?

Yes, in most cases it's reasonable to ask whether the terms are negotiable. Employer policies and circumstances vary, so the specific flexibility available will differ from one offer to the next.

### How much more salary should I ask for?

Your request should reflect the market range, the responsibilities of the role, your qualifications, and your own researched target, not a universal percentage that's assumed to apply to every negotiation.

### Can I negotiate a job offer after accepting it?

Reopening already-agreed terms is possible but tends to be more difficult. If something changes, review the agreement and raise it promptly and professionally rather than waiting.

### Should I negotiate salary by email or phone?

Either can work. Email gives you a written record of exactly what was discussed; a phone or video conversation can make it easier to clarify priorities and explore alternatives in real time. Whichever you use, confirm the final terms in writing afterward.

### What if the employer says the salary is non-negotiable?

Ask whether other components of the package, like a signing bonus, extra leave, or schedule flexibility, have room to move, without pressuring the employer further on the salary itself.

### Can negotiating a job offer cause an employer to withdraw it?

There's no universal guarantee of how any specific employer will respond. A respectful, evidence-based request is generally more constructive than an ultimatum, but it's worth weighing your own risk tolerance and circumstances before deciding how firmly to push.

### Can I use a competing offer to negotiate?

Yes, if it's genuine. Sharing honest details from a real competing offer can support your request, but a vague or exaggerated reference to other opportunities without specifics tends to undermine your credibility rather than strengthen your position.
