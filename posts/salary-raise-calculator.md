## What is a salary raise calculator?

A salary raise calculator is a tool that estimates your new earnings after a pay increase. Instead of multiplying and adding by hand, you enter your current salary and the raise percentage, and the calculator does the rest.

It helps you determine:

- **New salary amount:** what you will earn after the raise takes effect
- **Increase in earnings:** the exact dollar amount added to your pay
- **Percentage growth:** how large the raise is relative to your old salary
- **Annual income impact:** what the raise means over a full year

Here is a quick example:

```
Current Salary: $50,000
Raise:          10%
Raise Amount:   $5,000
New Salary:     $55,000
```

A 10% raise on $50,000 adds $5,000 per year. The calculation is the same whether you earn $30,000 or $300,000.

## How does a salary raise calculator work?

The calculator uses a simple two-step formula:

```
Raise Amount = Current Salary × Raise Percentage
New Salary   = Current Salary + Raise Amount
```

The only trick is to convert the percentage into a decimal first. Divide the percentage by 100: 8% becomes 0.08, 5% becomes 0.05, and 12.5% becomes 0.125.

```
Current Salary: $60,000
Raise:          8%

$60,000 × 0.08 = $4,800
New Salary = $60,000 + $4,800 = $64,800
```

**A faster shortcut:** multiply your salary by 1 plus the raise as a decimal. For an 8% raise, multiply by 1.08. Here, $60,000 × 1.08 = $64,800. This one-step version is what most online calculators do behind the scenes.

## Salary raise formulas

You do not need a calculator to understand the math. Three formulas cover almost every raise scenario.

**Formula 1: calculate the raise amount**

```
Raise Amount = Salary × Raise %
```

A 4% raise on $55,000 is $55,000 × 0.04 = $2,200.

**Formula 2: calculate the new salary**

```
New Salary = Current Salary + Raise Amount
```

Continuing the example, $55,000 + $2,200 = $57,200.

**Formula 3: calculate the raise percentage**

```
Raise % = (Increase ÷ Original Salary) × 100
```

Use this when your employer gives you a new salary figure but not the percentage. If your pay goes from $50,000 to $54,000, the increase is $4,000, and $4,000 ÷ $50,000 = 0.08, so the raise is 8%. For more worked examples, see [how to calculate the percentage of a salary increase](https://odojema.com/blog/how-to-calculate-percentage-of-salary-increase).

**Working backward: what raise do you need?** You can also use Formula 3 to find the raise required to reach a target. Suppose you earn $52,000 and want to reach $60,000:

```
Increase needed = $60,000 - $52,000 = $8,000
$8,000 ÷ $52,000 = 0.1538
Required raise  = about 15.4%
```

Knowing this tells you whether your goal is realistic for a standard annual review or whether it takes a promotion or a job change.

## How much is a raise worth per month?

Many employees focus on annual salary, but your monthly earnings are what you actually budget with. Breaking a raise into monthly amounts shows what it really changes.

```
Salary:             $50,000
Raise:              5%
Annual Increase:    $2,500
Monthly Increase:   $2,500 ÷ 12 = $208.33
New Monthly Pay:    $52,500 ÷ 12 = $4,375
```

If you are paid every two weeks (26 paychecks a year), divide by 26 instead:

```
Before the raise:    $50,000 ÷ 26 = $1,923.08
After the raise:     $52,500 ÷ 26 = $2,019.23
Increase per check:  about $96.15
```

All figures here are **gross**, meaning before taxes and deductions. Your take-home increase will be smaller. A larger raise can occasionally push part of your income into a higher tax bracket, but that only means the extra income is taxed at your marginal rate. It does not mean you take home less than before.

## Calculating a raise from hourly pay

If you are paid by the hour, the method is the same. Convert your hourly rate to an annual figure first, using the standard 2,080 working hours in a year (40 hours × 52 weeks).

```
Hourly Rate:         $20
Raise:               5%
Annual Pay Before:   $20 × 2,080 = $41,600
New Hourly Rate:     $20 × 1.05  = $21.00
Annual Pay After:    $21 × 2,080 = $43,680
Annual Increase:     $2,080
```

If your hours vary or you work part-time, replace 2,080 with the hours you actually work in a year. ## Why small raises add up over time

A single raise may look small, but raises compound. Each new salary becomes the base for the next raise. Here is $50,000 with a 4% raise every year:

```
Start:        $50,000.00
After Year 1: $52,000.00
After Year 2: $54,080.00
After Year 3: $56,243.20
```

After three years your salary is $6,243 higher, which is more than three separate 4% raises on the original $50,000 would give ($6,000). This is also why accepting a very small raise can cost more than it first appears: every future raise is calculated on a lower base. ## Common mistakes when calculating a raise

A calculator removes most arithmetic errors, but a few mistakes still happen:

- **Forgetting to convert the percentage.** Multiplying by 8 instead of 0.08 gives a result 100 times too large. Always divide the percentage by 100 first.
- **Using the wrong base.** The raise percentage applies to your current salary, not the new one. A 10% raise on $50,000 is $5,000, not $5,500.
- **Mixing gross and net pay.** Use gross (pre-tax) figures for accurate comparisons.
- **Ignoring inflation.** A 5% raise sounds good, but if prices rose 3%, your real gain is closer to 2%.
- **Comparing percentages without comparing amounts.** A 6% raise on $30,000 ($1,800) is smaller in dollars than a 3% raise on $80,000 ($2,400). Look at both numbers.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is a good salary raise percentage?**
A typical annual raise is 3% to 5%, while promotions often range from 10% to 20%. See [what is a good salary raise](https://odojema.com/blog/what-is-a-good-salary-raise) for a fuller breakdown.

**How do I calculate a raise manually?**
Multiply your current salary by the raise percentage (as a decimal), then add the result to your salary. A 6% raise on $45,000 is $45,000 × 0.06 = $2,700, so the new salary is $47,700. Our guide on [how to calculate a salary increase](https://odojema.com/blog/how-to-calculate-a-salary-increase) has more examples.

**Is a 10% raise good?**
For many employees, a 10% raise is considered strong and is often associated with promotions or exceptional performance. Read more in [is a 10% raise good?](https://odojema.com/blog/is-a-10-percent-raise-good).

**Is a 5% raise good?**
Yes, in most situations. It sits at the top of the standard annual range and usually keeps you ahead of inflation. See [is a 5% raise good?](https://odojema.com/blog/is-a-5-percent-raise-good).

**How much is a 5% raise on $60,000?**
A 5% raise equals $3,000, so the new salary is $63,000.

**Can I calculate raises using hourly pay?**
Yes. Convert your hourly earnings into annual pay first (hourly rate × 2,080 hours), then apply the raise percentage. You can also multiply your hourly rate directly by 1 plus the raise as a decimal. For a 5% raise on $20 per hour, that is $20 × 1.05 = $21 per hour.

**What is the difference between a raise amount and a raise percentage?**
The raise amount is the dollar value added to your pay, such as $3,000. The raise percentage is that amount as a share of your old salary, such as 5%. The calculator shows both so you can judge the raise in absolute and relative terms.
