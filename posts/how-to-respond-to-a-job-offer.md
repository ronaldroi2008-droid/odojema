To respond to a job offer, thank the employer, review the terms, and clearly state your decision. If you want the job but need better terms, negotiate before accepting. If you need more time, request an extension politely. If you decide the position is not right for you, decline professionally and express appreciation for the opportunity.

Receiving a job offer is an important step, but it doesn't mean you need to answer on the spot. Candidates often need time to review the terms before responding, and the appropriate response depends on whether the offer meets their expectations, needs negotiation, or simply isn't the right fit. This guide walks through four common paths, accept, negotiate, request more time, or decline, with ready-to-use wording for each.

## 1. What to Do Before Responding to a Job Offer

Before you commit to any response, take the time to actually review what's in front of you. That means looking closely at:

- Base salary and other compensation.
- Benefits, retirement contributions, and paid time off.
- Job responsibilities and reporting structure.
- Working hours, location, and flexibility.
- Start date and any stated conditions.
- The deadline for accepting or responding.

If anything in the offer is unclear, ask questions before making a final decision rather than guessing or assuming the best case. A rushed response, in either direction, is harder to walk back than a short delay spent getting clarity. If you haven't already worked through the offer in detail, it's worth first reading how to [evaluate a job offer](https://odojema.com/blog/how-to-evaluate-a-job-offer) across all ten factors, and how to [calculate the true value of a job offer](https://odojema.com/blog/true-value-of-a-job-offer) once benefits and work-related costs are factored in. Those steps give you the full picture this response is actually based on.

## 2. How to Accept a Job Offer Professionally

An acceptance message should be clear, positive, and consistent with the offer's actual terms. A strong acceptance:

- Thanks the employer.
- States clearly that you accept the offer.
- Confirms the position and agreed compensation, where appropriate.
- Confirms the expected start date.
- Asks about next steps and any onboarding documents you'll need.

A sample email:

> **Subject:** Acceptance of Job Offer — [Position Title]
>
> Dear [Hiring Manager's Name],
>
> Thank you for offering me the [Position Title] position at [Company Name]. I am pleased to accept the offer under the terms outlined in the offer letter.
>
> I look forward to joining the team on [Start Date]. Please let me know if there are any additional documents or steps I need to complete before my first day.
>
> Thank you again for this opportunity.
>
> Best regards,
> [Your Name]

Adjust this template to match the actual offer and any conditions that apply to your specific situation. If the offer letter references attachments, documents, or a formal signature process, mention that you're reviewing or completing those separately rather than treating the email alone as the final step.

## 3. How to Negotiate Before Accepting a Job Offer

If you want the position but aren't satisfied with one or more terms, it's reasonable to ask whether there's room for discussion before you accept. A solid approach includes:

- Researching a reasonable salary target based on the role and market.
- Supporting your request with your qualifications and relevant market data.
- Prioritizing the terms that matter most to you rather than negotiating everything at once.
- Maintaining a collaborative tone throughout the conversation.
- Waiting for written confirmation of any revised terms before treating them as agreed.

Sample wording:

> "Thank you for the offer. I am excited about the opportunity to join [Company Name]. Before making my final decision, I wanted to ask whether there is flexibility to discuss the base salary. Based on the responsibilities of the position and my relevant experience, I was hoping for [Target Salary]. I would welcome the opportunity to discuss this further."

This is only a starting point for the conversation. For the complete process, including how to build your case, respond to pushback, and negotiate benefits when salary isn't flexible, see how to [negotiate a better job offer](https://odojema.com/blog/how-to-negotiate-a-better-job-offer).

## 4. How to Ask for More Time to Consider a Job Offer

An extension can be appropriate when you need to review the offer more carefully, clarify benefits details, or complete another pending interview process elsewhere. When requesting more time:

- Ask as early as possible, ideally as soon as you know you'll need it.
- Propose a specific, reasonable response date rather than an open-ended delay.
- Acknowledge the employer's own timeline and constraints.

A sample email:

> **Subject:** Request for Additional Time — [Position Title]
>
> Dear [Hiring Manager's Name],
>
> Thank you for the offer for the [Position Title] role. I appreciate the opportunity and am carefully reviewing the details.
>
> Would it be possible to have until [Proposed Date] to provide my final response? I want to make an informed decision and give the opportunity the consideration it deserves.
>
> Thank you for your understanding. I look forward to hearing from you.
>
> Best regards,
> [Your Name]

The employer may or may not agree to an extension, and it's worth not assuming the deadline has moved until they actually confirm it. If you don't hear back promptly, a polite follow-up closer to the original deadline is reasonable.

## 5. How to Decline a Job Offer Politely

You can decline a job offer without providing a long explanation. The response should be prompt, respectful, and unambiguous, so the employer can move forward with their process. A good decline:

- Thanks the employer for the offer.
- Clearly states your decision to decline.
- Gives a brief explanation only if it feels appropriate to share.
- Maintains a professional relationship for the future.

A sample email:

> **Subject:** Job Offer — [Position Title]
>
> Dear [Hiring Manager's Name],
>
> Thank you for offering me the [Position Title] position at [Company Name]. I appreciate the time you and the team invested in the interview process.
>
> After careful consideration, I have decided to decline the offer. This was not an easy decision, and I am grateful for the opportunity to learn more about your organization.
>
> Thank you again for your consideration. I wish you and the team continued success.
>
> Best regards,
> [Your Name]

You're not obligated to explain your reasoning in detail, and a vague but gracious explanation, or none at all, is entirely acceptable. What matters most is that the decision is communicated clearly and promptly, rather than left ambiguous or delayed.

## 6. What If You Are Waiting for Another Job Offer?

Sometimes you'd prefer a different opportunity, but the offer in hand comes with a deadline that arrives before the other process has concluded. A few things help in this situation:

- Ask the current employer for a reasonable extension, explaining honestly that you're finalizing a decision.
- Contact the other employer to ask about the status of their hiring process and a realistic timeline.
- Compare the offers using consistent criteria once you have enough information from both. For a structured way to do this, see how to [compare two job offers](https://odojema.com/blog/how-to-compare-job-offers) side by side.
- Avoid making false claims about competing offers to either employer; honesty here protects your reputation even if it feels like it costs you leverage in the moment.
- Make your decision based on the actual information available to you rather than assuming a better offer will arrive in time.

Neither employer is obligated to accommodate your preferred timeline, and it's worth going into these conversations with that understood. A respectful, honest request for more time is reasonable; an indefinite delay is not something most employers will wait out.

## 7. Common Job Offer Response Mistakes

A handful of avoidable errors can create confusion or unnecessary risk during this stage:

- Accepting before understanding important terms of the offer.
- Ignoring the employer's stated response deadline.
- Negotiating without a clear request or rationale behind it.
- Sending an unclear acceptance or rejection that leaves the employer guessing.
- Assuming a requested extension has been approved before hearing back.
- Relying on verbal changes to the offer without getting written confirmation.
- Making commitments, to this employer or another, that conflict with the actual terms of the offer.
- Disappearing without responding at all, which burns the relationship far more than a clear decline ever would.

## How Response Timing Affects Your Options

The amount of time you take to respond can itself shape which options remain available to you. Responding very quickly, within a day or two, generally preserves the most flexibility: there's still time to ask clarifying questions, request a small extension if something unexpected comes up, or open a negotiation conversation without feeling rushed.

Waiting until close to the employer's deadline narrows your options. A negotiation request made the day before a response is due can come across as last-minute even when your reasoning is sound, and there's less room for back-and-forth discussion if the employer needs a final answer immediately. If you know you'll need to negotiate, ask for clarity on timing, or request more time, it's generally better to start that conversation as early as reasonably possible rather than waiting until the deadline is close to force a decision.

## How to Handle a Verbal Offer Before the Written One Arrives

It's increasingly common to receive a verbal offer, over the phone or in a video call, before the formal written offer letter follows. It's reasonable to express enthusiasm and general interest at that stage, but hold off on a final acceptance, negotiation, or decline until you've seen the terms in writing. Verbal offers can be summarized imprecisely, and the written offer is what actually documents salary, start date, and other conditions you'd be agreeing to.

If a verbal offer comes with pressure for an immediate verbal commitment, it's reasonable to say something like: "I'm very interested and looking forward to reviewing the written offer so I can respond formally." This keeps the relationship positive while protecting you from agreeing to terms you haven't actually seen documented yet.

## What to Do If the Written Offer Differs From What Was Discussed

Occasionally, the written offer doesn't match what was discussed verbally, whether that's a different salary figure, a changed start date, or a benefit that was mentioned but doesn't appear in the documentation. This is worth raising directly and promptly, rather than assuming it was intentional or letting it go unaddressed.

A straightforward way to raise it: "I noticed the written offer shows [detail] rather than [what was discussed]. Could you help me understand the difference?" This gives the employer a chance to correct a genuine error or clarify their reasoning, without accusing them of bad faith. How they respond to this kind of question is also useful information about how the company handles discrepancies in general, which is itself worth factoring into your decision.

## How to Negotiate a Signing Bonus or Relocation Package

Not every offer has room to move on base salary, but a signing bonus or relocation package is sometimes easier for an employer to adjust, since it's typically a one-time cost rather than a change to ongoing payroll. If base salary feels fixed but you still want to improve the offer, it's worth asking about these separately rather than assuming the whole package is locked.

A few things to know before asking:

- **Signing bonuses often come with a payback clause.** Many employers require you to repay some or all of a signing bonus if you leave before a set period, commonly one year. Ask directly whether this applies and get the specific terms in writing before accepting.
- **Relocation packages vary widely in what they actually cover.** Some include a flat stipend, others cover specific costs like moving companies, temporary housing, or a house-hunting trip, and some are structured as reimbursement rather than upfront payment. Ask exactly what's included before comparing it to a cash alternative.
- **A signing bonus doesn't replace a base salary gap.** It's a one-time payment, while base salary compounds over every future raise, bonus calculation, and retirement contribution tied to a percentage of salary. If an employer offers a larger signing bonus instead of moving on base salary, weigh that trade-off deliberately rather than treating the numbers as equivalent.

Sample wording for this specific ask: "I understand there may be limited flexibility on base salary. Would the company be able to offer a signing bonus, or additional relocation support, to help bridge the difference?" This keeps the conversation collaborative and gives the employer an alternative lever to work with if salary truly is fixed.

## What to Know Before Rescinding an Accepted Job Offer

Occasionally, a candidate accepts an offer and then needs to back out, whether because a better opportunity appears, personal circumstances change, or something about the role turns out differently than expected once more details emerge. This is a sensitive situation, and it's worth handling carefully.

In most of the United States, employment is at-will, meaning either the employer or the employee can generally end the relationship at any time, including before a start date. That said, whether rescinding an acceptance carries any formal consequence depends on what was actually signed, whether a separate employment contract exists, and the specific jurisdiction involved. This is general information, not legal advice, and if you've signed anything beyond a standard offer letter, it's worth reviewing those terms directly or asking a qualified professional before proceeding.

Beyond the legal question, there's a practical and relationship one. Notify the employer as early as possible once you've decided, rather than waiting. A short, direct, and professional message tends to preserve the relationship better than a long justification:

> "After further consideration, I've decided to withdraw my acceptance of the [Position Title] offer. I recognize this creates inconvenience, and I apologize for that. I appreciated the opportunity to get to know the team."

This isn't a step to take lightly or repeatedly. Industries and professional networks are often smaller than they appear, and a pattern of accepting and then rescinding offers can follow a candidate's reputation. If you're genuinely unsure, it's usually better to ask for more time before accepting in the first place, using the request-for-time approach described above, rather than accepting and reconsidering afterward.

## How to Respond to a Counteroffer From Your Current Employer

A related but distinct situation arises when you have a new offer in hand and your current employer responds with a counteroffer to try to keep you. This isn't quite the same as choosing between two external offers, since staying also means navigating whatever led you to look for a new role in the first place.

A few things worth thinking through before deciding:

- **Ask why you were looking in the first place.** If the reasons were primarily about compensation, a counteroffer that fixes the pay gap may genuinely resolve the issue. If the reasons included management, growth opportunities, workload, or company direction, a salary increase alone usually doesn't fix those underlying problems.
- **Consider how the relationship changes after a counteroffer.** Some employees find that once a manager knows they were ready to leave, the working relationship shifts, whether through reduced trust, a different view of their long-term commitment, or simply awkwardness that doesn't fully go away.
- **Compare the counteroffer using the same criteria as any other offer.** Total compensation, growth trajectory, and day-to-day role quality all matter here just as much as they would when comparing two new external offers. A salary bump at your current company doesn't automatically outweigh a meaningfully better role elsewhere.
- **Decide before you're in the room.** Counteroffer conversations can be emotionally charged, especially if a manager you respect is asking you to stay. Deciding your actual priorities in advance, away from that pressure, usually leads to a clearer decision than deciding in the moment.

There's no universal right answer here. Some employees accept a counteroffer and are glad they did; others find the original reasons for leaving resurface within a year. What matters is that the decision is based on the actual underlying reasons you were considering a change, not just on whichever number is larger.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How long should I take to respond to a job offer?

Follow the deadline stated by the employer. If you need more time, ask for an extension promptly and wait for confirmation rather than assuming it has been granted.

### Should I accept a job offer immediately?

Not necessarily. Review the written terms and clarify important questions before accepting, particularly if you need to negotiate or compare other opportunities first.

### Can I negotiate after receiving a written offer?

Yes, you can ask whether the employer is open to discussing terms before accepting. Whether changes are actually possible depends on the specific employer and circumstances.

### Is it okay to decline a job offer by email?

Yes. A concise, respectful email is generally appropriate, unless the employer has specifically requested another communication method.

### How do I ask for time without losing the job offer?

Express continued interest, explain that you need time to review the details, and propose a reasonable date. There's no guarantee the employer will extend the deadline, but a clear, professional request is the best approach available.

### Should I explain why I am declining a job offer?

A brief explanation is optional in many situations. You can simply thank the employer and state your decision without disclosing private details about your reasoning.

### Can I change my mind after accepting a job offer?

Changing your mind may have professional or contractual consequences depending on the agreement and your specific circumstances. Review the relevant terms carefully and communicate promptly if your situation changes.

### What should I do if two job offers have overlapping deadlines?

Ask each employer for a short, specific extension rather than guessing under pressure, and compare the offers using the same criteria once you have enough information from both. Most employers would rather grant a brief extension than lose a candidate to a rushed decision.
