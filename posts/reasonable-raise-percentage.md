A reasonable raise percentage to ask for depends on your situation, but a useful way to find your number is to check it against three benchmarks: the average raise most people in your position receive, the inflation-adjusted raise needed to maintain your purchasing power, and the market-rate raise needed to match what your role currently pays elsewhere. A request that clears all three is hard to argue with; a request that clears only one is still worth making, but it helps to know which benchmark is actually doing the work.

Asking for a raise without a target number, or asking for a number pulled from a general sense of "wanting more," tends to produce weaker outcomes than a specific ask grounded in something concrete. This guide walks through how to find a reasonable number using the three-benchmark framework, how to build the case behind it, and how to time the ask.

## The three benchmarks for a reasonable ask

Before deciding what number to request, check it against each of these:

1. **The average raise benchmark.** What does a typical raise look like for someone in your role, industry, and company size? This tells you whether your ask is in line with what's commonly granted, or well above it.
2. **The inflation benchmark.** What percentage increase do you need just to maintain your current purchasing power? A raise below this number is a real pay cut even if the number on paper looks positive.
3. **The market-rate benchmark.** What does your specific role currently pay at other companies? If your pay has fallen behind the market, even a raise that beats the average and beats inflation might not be enough to close that gap.

A raise can clear one of these bars and miss the others entirely. A 4% raise beats a 2% inflation rate but might still leave you below market if your role's pay has shifted significantly since you were last hired or promoted. Checking all three, rather than relying on just one, is what makes an ask reasonable rather than arbitrary.

## How to find your number using these three benchmarks

A practical process for landing on a specific percentage to request:

1. **Research your market-rate benchmark first.** Compensation data for your specific role, location, and experience level is the most concrete anchor you can bring to a conversation, more persuasive than a general sense that "raises should be bigger."
2. **Check the current inflation rate** for your region and use it as your floor. Anything below this number doesn't represent real growth, which is a reasonable thing to point out in the conversation.
3. **Compare both to what you already know about typical raises** at your company or in your industry, so your ask doesn't land as a surprise even if it's on the higher end of what's typical.
4. **Pick a number slightly above your actual target**, within reason, since negotiations often settle somewhere between the initial ask and the initial offer. Asking for exactly what you'd be satisfied with leaves no room for that back-and-forth.

This process tends to produce a number that's defensible in conversation, rather than one that sounds reasonable only in your own head.

## How to build your case for that number

A specific percentage lands differently depending on what's behind it. A few things worth bringing to the conversation:

- **Documented performance**, ideally with measurable results rather than general impressions of a good year.
- **Expanded responsibilities** that weren't part of your role when your current salary was set.
- **Market data** for your specific role, title, and location, pulled from a credible source rather than informal impressions.
- **Timing tied to a natural moment**, such as a performance review, a project's successful completion, or a new responsibility being formalized.

Avoid leading with comparisons to a specific coworker's raise or salary, since that information is rarely complete and can come across as adversarial rather than evidence-based. Your own performance, market data, and responsibilities make a stronger, more professional case on their own.

## When a smaller ask is still the reasonable one

Not every situation calls for pushing toward the top of a benchmark range. If your company is navigating a genuinely difficult financial period, layoffs are affecting your industry, or you've recently received a substantial raise tied to a promotion, a more modest ask, one that still clears the inflation benchmark but doesn't aggressively chase the market-rate ceiling, can be the more strategically sound choice. Pushing hard for a maximum number in a context where it's unlikely to be approved can cost you credibility for a future ask when conditions improve.

It also helps to separate "what would make this raise good" from "what's reasonable to request right now." A modest raise can still be entirely reasonable given your company's situation, even if it falls short of what the three benchmarks would otherwise support. ## Timing your ask

When you raise the conversation matters almost as much as the number itself. A request made right before a scheduled performance review, after successfully completing a major project, or when you've just taken on new responsibilities tends to land better than one raised at an arbitrary point in the year. If your company has a formal review cycle, it's generally more effective to prepare your case ahead of that cycle than to request an off-cycle conversation without a specific trigger, though a significant market shift or a clearly outdated salary can justify asking outside the normal cycle.

If the conversation is tied specifically to a promotion rather than a routine review, the dynamics shift somewhat, since the raise is attached to a new role rather than continued performance in the same one. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### What percentage should I ask for in a raise?

It depends on your situation, but checking your number against the average raise for your role, the current inflation rate, and your market rate gives you a defensible target rather than an arbitrary one.

### Is it reasonable to ask for a 10% raise?

It can be, particularly if your market-rate research shows your pay has fallen meaningfully behind, or if the request is tied to a promotion with significantly expanded responsibilities. For a routine annual review with no major change in role, 10% is on the higher end and worth backing with specific evidence.

### Should I tell my employer what other companies pay for my role?

Yes, in general terms. Referencing market data, without necessarily naming a specific competing offer, is a common and reasonable part of a compensation conversation, and most employers expect this kind of research to come up.

### What if my employer says no to my ask?

Ask what would need to be true for the number to be reconsidered, and when. A clear answer gives you a concrete path forward; a vague one is useful information about how seriously the request was actually considered.

### Is it better to ask for a specific percentage or a specific dollar amount?

Either can work, but a specific number, in either form, is more effective than a vague request for "more." If you're unsure which to lead with, a dollar figure tends to be easier for both sides to evaluate quickly, while a percentage is easier to compare against benchmarks.
