To calculate percentage increase, subtract the original value from the new value, divide the difference by the original value, and multiply the result by 100. This formula is commonly used to calculate salary raises, revenue growth, investment gains, and price increases.

```
Percentage Increase = ((New Value − Original Value) ÷ Original Value) × 100
```

## What is percentage increase?

Percentage increase measures how much a value has grown compared to its starting amount. It converts a raw difference, like an extra $5,000 in salary or an extra $20 in price, into a percentage that's easy to compare across different starting values.

This makes it useful across a wide range of situations:

- **Salary raises:** How much more you're earning compared to before.
- **Revenue growth:** How much a company's income has grown year over year.
- **Sales increases:** How much more product or service volume has sold compared to a previous period.
- **Investment returns:** How much an investment has grown in value.
- **Price changes:** How much more a product or service costs than it used to.

Here's a simple example of what percentage increase looks like in practice:

```
$50,000 → $55,000
```

The value increased by **10%**. The rest of this guide shows exactly how that number is calculated.

## Percentage increase formula

The full formula has three parts:

```
Percentage Increase = ((New Value − Original Value) ÷ Original Value) × 100
```

**Original value.** This is the starting amount, before any change. In a salary example, it's your old salary. In a price example, it's the previous price.

**New value.** This is the ending amount, after the change has occurred. It's your new salary, the new price, or the updated figure you're comparing against the original.

**Difference.** This is simply new value minus original value, the raw amount of increase before it's converted into a percentage.

Once you have the difference, dividing it by the original value and multiplying by 100 converts that raw number into a percentage you can compare across different situations, regardless of how large or small the original value was.

## Step-by-step example

Here's the full process broken into three steps, using a salary raise as the example.

**Example salary raise**

```
Original Salary: $50,000
New Salary:      $55,000
```

### Step 1: Find the difference

Subtract the original value from the new value:

```
55,000 − 50,000 = 5,000
```

### Step 2: Divide by the original value

Divide that difference by the original value:

```
5,000 ÷ 50,000 = 0.10
```

### Step 3: Multiply by 100

Multiply the result by 100 to convert it into a percentage:

```
0.10 × 100 = 10%
```

**Result: 10% increase.** The salary grew from $50,000 to $55,000, which represents a 10% increase over the original amount.

## How to calculate a salary raise percentage

The formula works exactly the same way when applied specifically to a raise. Here's an example:

```
Current Salary: $48,000
New Salary:     $52,800
```

**Calculation:**

```
(52,800 − 48,000) ÷ 48,000 × 100
```

Working through each step:

```
52,800 − 48,000 = 4,800
4,800 ÷ 48,000 = 0.10
0.10 × 100 = 10%
```

## More percentage increase examples by category

The formula stays exactly the same no matter what the numbers represent. Here's how it applies across a few common real-world categories beyond salary.

### Revenue growth

A business comparing this year's revenue to last year's can use the same three-step process:

```
Last Year's Revenue: $100,000
This Year's Revenue: $125,000

Step 1: 125,000 − 100,000 = 25,000
Step 2: 25,000 ÷ 100,000 = 0.25
Step 3: 0.25 × 100 = 25%
```

**Result: 25% revenue growth.** This figure is one of the most common metrics businesses track year over year, and it uses the exact same formula as a salary raise.

### Price increase

If a product's price rises, the same formula shows exactly how much more expensive it's become in percentage terms:

```
Old Price: $40
New Price: $46

Step 1: 46 − 40 = 6
Step 2: 6 ÷ 40 = 0.15
Step 3: 0.15 × 100 = 15%
```

**Result: 15% price increase.** This kind of calculation is useful for comparing price changes across products with very different starting prices, since a $6 increase means something very different on a $40 item than it would on a $400 item.

### Investment growth

An investment that grows in value over time can be measured the same way:

```
Initial Investment: $2,000
Current Value:      $2,300

Step 1: 2,300 − 2,000 = 300
Step 2: 300 ÷ 2,000 = 0.15
Step 3: 0.15 × 100 = 15%
```

**Result: 15% investment growth.** Whether the underlying value is a salary, a price, revenue, or an investment, the formula itself never changes, only the two numbers you plug in.

## Quick ways to estimate percentage increase without a calculator

Sometimes you just need a fast, rough estimate rather than an exact figure. A few mental shortcuts can help.

**Round to friendly numbers.** If your original value is close to a round number, like $49,500 instead of $50,000, rounding first makes the mental math easier and still gets you very close to the accurate answer.

**Use 10% as a building block.** Finding 10% of a number is simple: just move the decimal point one place to the left. From there, you can estimate other percentages by scaling that 10% figure up or down. For example, 5% is half of 10%, and 20% is double 10%.

**Check your answer against the benchmarks.** If you calculate a raise and the result seems unusually high or low compared to the common benchmarks covered earlier in this guide, it's worth double-checking your math, particularly the new-value-as-base mistake covered in the next section, which is the most common source of errors.

## Percentage increase vs percentage change

"Percentage increase" and "percentage change" are related but not identical, and it's worth understanding the distinction.

**Percentage increase** is used specifically when a value grows. For example:

```
$100 → $120
```

This represents a straightforward percentage increase, calculated with the same formula covered above.

**Percentage decrease** is used when a value falls instead of growing. For example:

```
$100 → $80
```

Here, the value has gone down, not up. The underlying formula is the same, (New Value − Original Value) ÷ Original Value × 100, but the result comes out negative, which signals a decrease rather than an increase. In this example, the calculation gives −20%, reflecting a 20% decrease rather than a 20% gain.

"Percentage change" is the broader term that covers both cases, increases and decreases alike, while "percentage increase" specifically refers to situations where the value has grown. Keeping this distinction in mind helps avoid confusion when a result comes out negative and you're not sure whether that reflects an error or simply a decrease.

## Common mistakes when calculating percentage increase

A few recurring errors come up when people calculate percentage increase by hand.

**Mistake 1: Using the new value as the base.** The original value, not the new value, always goes in the denominator. Using the wrong base number gives an inaccurate result:

```
Incorrect: 5,000 ÷ 55,000
Correct:   5,000 ÷ 50,000
```

Dividing by the new value instead of the original understates the percentage increase, since the denominator is larger than it should be.

**Mistake 2: Forgetting to multiply by 100.** Dividing the difference by the original value gives a decimal, like 0.10, not a percentage. Skipping the final step of multiplying by 100 leaves the answer in the wrong format, 0.10 instead of 10%.

**Mistake 3: Mixing increase and decrease calculations.** When comparing two values, it's easy to accidentally treat a decrease as an increase, or vice versa, especially when working through several numbers in a row. Double-checking which value is larger, the original or the new, before applying the formula helps avoid sign errors in the final result.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the formula for percentage increase?**
Percentage Increase = ((New Value − Original Value) ÷ Original Value) × 100.

**How do I calculate a salary increase percentage?**
Subtract your old salary from your new salary, divide by the old salary, then multiply by 100. For example, a raise from $48,000 to $52,800 works out to a 10% increase.

**How much is a 10% raise?**
A 10% raise on a $50,000 salary equals $5,000, resulting in a new salary of $55,000.

**What is a good percentage increase?**
A good percentage increase depends on the situation, industry, and goals. Salary increases vary across organizations and roles, with routine annual raises often falling between 2% and 5%, while promotions or market adjustments can run higher.

**Can I use percentage increase for prices and revenue?**
Yes. Percentage increase calculations work for salaries, prices, revenue, investments, and many other financial values, since the formula only depends on an original value and a new value, not on what those values represent.
