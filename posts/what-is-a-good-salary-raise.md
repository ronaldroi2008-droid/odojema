A good salary raise depends on why it's being given, not just the percentage on its own. A cost-of-living adjustment of 2-4% is normal and fine for what it is, a merit increase of 3-5% signals solid performance, a promotion raise of 5-15% reflects new responsibilities, and a raise tied to a job change or in-demand skills can reasonably run much higher. Judging a raise without knowing its source is like judging a grade without knowing the test.

Context always matters more than the raw percentage. The same 5% raise can be disappointing for someone switching employers and excellent for someone staying put through a routine annual review. This guide walks through typical ranges by source, a simple way to judge your own raise, and what a "good" raise actually looks like once it hits your paycheck.

## Why "good" depends on where the raise is coming from

Raises aren't one category of event. Employers grant them for different reasons, and each reason comes with its own reasonable range:

- **Cost-of-living adjustments** exist to keep your pay roughly level with inflation, not to reward performance. A 2-4% cost-of-living raise doing exactly that job is working as intended, even though the number looks modest next to a promotion raise.
- **Merit raises** reward performance within your current role. These typically land around 3-5% and reflect how your results compared to expectations during the review period.
- **Promotion raises** come with a new title and expanded responsibilities, which is why they run meaningfully higher, typically 5-15%, than a raise for staying in the same role.
- **Job-change raises** are priced against the external market rather than your current employer's internal pay bands, which is why switching companies often produces a bigger jump, commonly 10-25% or more, than staying and waiting for an annual review.
- **High-demand-skill raises** come from leverage rather than a fixed category. If your skills are scarce and in demand, a raise can run 15-30% or higher, driven by what the market is actually willing to pay to keep or hire you.

Comparing a cost-of-living raise to a promotion raise and concluding one is "better" misses the point. They're answering different questions, and a modest cost-of-living adjustment isn't a bad raise just because a promotion raise looks bigger.

## Typical raise ranges by source

| Raise type | Typical range | What it's actually rewarding |
|---|---|---|
| Cost-of-living adjustment | 2%-4% | Keeping pace with inflation, not performance |
| Merit increase (annual review) | 3%-5% | Performance within your current role |
| Promotion | 5%-15% | New title, expanded responsibilities |
| Job change | 10%-25%+ | External market rate for the role |
| High-demand skills | 15%-30%+ | Leverage and scarcity, not a fixed category |

These ranges are general benchmarks, not guarantees, and they shift with company size, industry, and the broader labor market. ## How to judge your own raise

Rather than asking "is X% good" in the abstract, a more useful process walks through four questions in order:

1. **What triggered this raise?** Identify whether it's a cost-of-living adjustment, a merit increase, a promotion, or something else. This determines which range above actually applies to you.
2. **Where does it fall in that range?** A 4% merit increase and a 4% promotion raise aren't equally strong, even though the number is identical, because they're being measured against different typical ranges.
3. **Does it beat inflation?** A raise only represents real growth once it clears the current inflation rate. A 3% raise against 4% inflation is a real pay cut of roughly 1%, even though the number on paper went up.
4. **How does it compare to market rate for your role?** A raise can look generous against your own raise history and still leave you below what your role currently pays elsewhere. Checking both is worth the extra step.

A raise that clears all four checks is a strong one. A raise that clears only the first two, reasonable for its source but behind inflation or below market, is worth a closer look before you decide how satisfied to be with it.

## Common mistakes when judging a raise

A handful of comparison habits tend to distort how people feel about a raise that might actually be reasonable for what it is:

- **Comparing your raise to a coworker's without context.** Different roles, performance ratings, and starting salaries make a side-by-side percentage comparison misleading on its own.
- **Ignoring total compensation.** A raise that looks modest in base salary can come with improved bonus eligibility, equity, or benefits that add real value beyond the percentage. For the full picture, see what's included in [total compensation](https://odojema.com/blog/what-is-total-compensation).
- **Assuming last year's percentage sets this year's standard.** Company performance, inflation, and your own role can all change year to year, so there's no reason one year's raise should predict the next.
- **Negotiating after a raise is already finalized.** Raise conversations are far more effective before numbers are locked in than after, particularly ahead of a scheduled review or promotion decision.

## When a modest raise can still be the right outcome

A raise that lands at the low end of its typical range isn't automatically a problem to fix. If a company is navigating a difficult year financially, a smaller-than-usual merit increase that still clears inflation can be a reasonable outcome, especially if layoffs or hiring freezes are affecting peers elsewhere in your industry. Context cuts both ways: the same due diligence that tells you when to push for more should also tell you when a modest number is actually fair given the circumstances.

If you're specifically weighing whether to push for a bigger number, it helps to separate "is this raise good for what it is" from "should I ask for more." The first question is answered by the ranges and checks above; the second depends on your specific leverage, timing, and what you're prepared to ask for, which is a related but different decision.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is a 5% raise good?

It depends on the source. A 5% merit increase during an annual review is generally strong. A 5% raise for a promotion or job change is on the low end for those categories, since both typically run higher.

### Is a 10% raise good?

Usually, yes. A 10% raise commonly reflects a promotion, a retention effort, or a response to a competing offer, and it's uncommon for a routine annual cost-of-living or merit adjustment.

### What's considered a bad raise?

A raise that falls below inflation, fails to account for substantially expanded responsibilities, or leaves your pay well below the market rate for your role are all signs worth examining further, regardless of what the percentage itself looks like.

### Should I compare my raise to the national average?

A national average is a useful reference point, but it isn't tailored to your industry, role, location, or the specific reason behind your raise. Use it as one data point among several rather than the deciding factor.

### How much should I ask for in a raise?

This depends heavily on the situation. For annual reviews, 3-7% is a reasonable starting ask; for promotions, 10-20%; and for job changes, 15-30% isn't unusual when backed by market data and your specific qualifications.
