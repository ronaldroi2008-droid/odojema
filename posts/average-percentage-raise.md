The average annual raise in the United States is commonly cited as around 3%, with most routine raises falling between 2% and 5%. That single figure is useful as a reference point, but it's also easy to misread, since a reported average can be pulled upward by a relatively small number of large raises, promotions, and market adjustments, in ways that don't reflect what a typical employee actually receives.

This guide focuses on the numbers themselves: what "average" actually measures, what pulls it in one direction or another, what it looks like in real dollars, and how to use it as a reference point without over-relying on a single figure.

## What counts as "the average"

When a source reports an average raise, it's almost always describing the mean, the total of all raises divided by the number of people who received one, rather than the median, the raise that sits exactly in the middle of the distribution. These two numbers can diverge meaningfully. If most employees receive a modest 2-3% increase but a smaller group receives 15-20% raises tied to promotions or retention efforts, the mean gets pulled upward even though the typical, most common experience is closer to the lower end.

Average figures also vary by source. Government labor statistics, private payroll data, and industry salary surveys each use different sample sizes, time periods, and employee populations, which is why you'll sometimes see slightly different average-raise figures reported in different places for roughly the same period.

## Why a single average number can be misleading

A headline average answers "what's the mean raise across everyone," not "what should I expect." A few things specifically skew it:

- **A small number of large raises pulls the mean up.** Promotions, retention counteroffers, and market corrections tend to be considerably larger than routine annual increases, and even a modest share of these in the dataset raises the overall average.
- **Industry mix matters.** An average blended across industries with very different typical raise sizes tells you less than an average specific to your own industry.
- **Company size matters.** Larger companies with more standardized compensation structures often cluster more tightly around a predictable number, while smaller companies and startups show more variation in either direction.
- **The reporting period matters.** Averages calculated during a tight labor market, when employers compete harder for talent, tend to run higher than averages from a period of broader economic slowdown.

None of this means the average figure is wrong, just that it's a single summary statistic standing in for a much wider spread of actual outcomes.

## Average raise in dollars

A percentage is easier to reason about with an actual dollar figure attached. Here's what a 3% raise looks like in dollar terms at a few common salary levels:

The percentage gap between these numbers looks small on paper, but the dollar gap compounds meaningfully over time as each raise builds on the previous year's higher salary.

## How the average moves year to year

The average raise isn't a fixed number; it shifts with broader economic conditions. A few of the biggest drivers:

- **Inflation.** Employers often factor the current inflation rate into planned increases, particularly for cost-of-living adjustments, which can push the overall average up or down in step with inflation trends.
- **Labor market tightness.** When employers are competing harder to retain employees, average raises, and the number of larger retention-driven raises specifically, tend to rise.
- **Overall economic conditions.** A strong economic period with healthy corporate earnings tends to support larger average increases; a period of broader economic uncertainty tends to compress them.

Because of this, an average figure from a single year is really only useful if you know roughly when it was measured and under what conditions, rather than treating it as a constant across time.

## Average raise vs inflation

Whether an average raise represents real growth depends entirely on how it compares to inflation during the same period. A 3% average raise during a year with 2% inflation represents roughly 1% of real purchasing-power growth. The same 3% average raise during a year with 5% inflation represents a real loss of roughly 2%, even though the raise percentage itself didn't change. Tracking the gap between the average raise and the inflation rate for the same period gives a more complete picture than looking at either number alone.

## How to use the average as a reference point

The average raise figure is most useful as one data point among several, not as a target or a verdict on your own raise. A practical way to use it:

1. **Note the average for context**, but look specifically for figures broken down by your industry and role when they're available, since a blended national average can differ meaningfully from what's typical in your specific field.
2. **Compare your raise to the average, then compare it to inflation separately.** A raise that beats the average but trails inflation still represents a real-terms loss.
3. **Treat a raise well above the average as a signal to understand why**, whether that's a promotion, a retention effort, or a market correction, rather than assuming it's simply generous.
4. **Treat a raise below the average as a prompt to look deeper**, not an automatic verdict, since your specific situation, company performance, and review timing all affect where you land relative to a broad average.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the average raise percentage in a typical year?

Commonly cited figures put the average annual raise around 3%, with most routine increases falling between 2% and 5%, though the exact figure shifts year to year with inflation and labor market conditions.

### Is the average raise the same as the median raise?

Not necessarily. The average (mean) can be pulled upward by a relatively small number of large raises, while the median reflects the raise that sits in the middle of the full distribution, which is often a more representative figure for what a typical employee receives.

### Why do different sources report different average raise figures?

Different data sources use different sample sizes, time periods, and employee populations, which naturally produces somewhat different averages even when measuring roughly the same thing.

### Does the average raise account for inflation?

No, the raw average raise percentage is a nominal figure. Whether it represents real growth depends on comparing it separately to the inflation rate for the same period.

### Is my raise bad if it's below the average?

Not automatically. A below-average raise can still be reasonable depending on your company's performance, your role, and the broader circumstances behind the number.
