The raise associated with a promotion varies by employer, industry, job level, and responsibilities. Promotion-related salary increases are often larger than routine annual raises because they reflect increased responsibilities, new skills, leadership duties, or movement into a higher-paying position.

This guide covers what counts as a promotion raise, how to calculate one, why the size varies so much, and how it compares with the routine annual raise you'd get in a regular review.

## What is a promotion raise?

A promotion raise is a salary increase received when moving into a higher-level role. A few common examples of the kind of move that triggers this type of raise:

- Team Member → Supervisor
- Supervisor → Manager
- Manager → Director
- Junior Developer → Senior Developer

Unlike a standard annual raise, a promotion raise typically reflects expanded responsibilities rather than simply rewarding performance in the same role. The employer is effectively pricing a new, more demanding job, not just adjusting pay for the one you already had.

## Promotion raise vs annual raise

These two types of raises serve different purposes, even though both result in a bigger paycheck.

| Type | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Annual Raise | Regular salary adjustment |
| Promotion Raise | Compensation for higher responsibilities |

Promotion raises are often larger because the employee is taking on a more advanced role, with new duties, often more decision-making authority, and sometimes people management responsibilities that didn't exist in the previous position. An annual raise, by contrast, usually keeps you in the same role and simply adjusts your pay within that role's existing range.

## Example promotion raise calculations

Here are two worked examples showing how the math plays out at different salary levels.

**Example 1**

```
Current Salary: $50,000
New Salary:     $55,000

Calculation: (($55,000 − $50,000) ÷ $50,000) × 100
Result: 10% Promotion Raise
```

**Example 2**

```
Current Salary: $60,000
New Salary:     $69,000

Result: 15% Promotion Raise
```

These two examples land within the range commonly associated with promotions, generally higher than a routine annual increase but varying quite a bit depending on the specific move.

## How to calculate a promotion raise percentage

The formula is the same one used for any percentage increase:

```
Promotion Raise Percentage = ((New Salary − Old Salary) ÷ Old Salary) × 100
```

**Step-by-step example:**

```
Old Salary: $48,000
New Salary: $54,000

Difference: $54,000 − $48,000 = $6,000
$6,000 ÷ $48,000 = 0.125
0.125 × 100 = 12.5%
```

## Why promotion raises vary

There's no single standard percentage for a promotion raise, and several factors explain why the number can differ so much from one situation to the next.

**Industry.** Some industries have larger salary ranges between job levels than others. A promotion in a field with wide pay bands between junior and senior roles can result in a much bigger jump than the same type of move in an industry with flatter, more compressed pay scales.

**Company size.** Large organizations may have structured promotion pay bands, with defined salary ranges tied to specific job levels or titles. Smaller companies may have more flexibility, which can mean either smaller or larger increases depending on the individual situation and the company's budget.

**Job level.** Moving into leadership roles may result in larger increases than a promotion between two individual contributor roles, since leadership responsibilities often come with a more significant jump in scope, accountability, and compensation structure.

**Market demand.** Skills that are difficult to replace may command higher salaries, and a promotion into a role requiring scarce or highly sought-after expertise can come with a larger increase than a promotion into a role where qualified candidates are easier to find.

## Is a promotion raise better than an annual raise?

A promotion raise and an annual raise serve different purposes, so "better" depends on what you're comparing them for.

A promotion raise usually reflects:

- Higher responsibilities
- Increased expectations
- Leadership duties
- Expanded decision-making authority

An annual raise is generally tied to performance reviews or routine compensation adjustments, without necessarily changing what the job itself involves. In dollar terms, a promotion raise is typically larger, since it compensates for a meaningfully different role rather than incremental performance in the same one. But a promotion also usually means more responsibility and a higher bar for what's expected of you going forward, so the comparison isn't purely about which number is bigger.

## How promotion raises affect future earnings

Promotion raises can compound over time, the same way any raise does, but the effect tends to be more pronounced because the increase itself is often larger.

```
Current Salary   = $50,000
Promotion Raise  = 10%
New Salary       = $55,000
```

Future raises may be based on $55,000 rather than $50,000. This means every subsequent annual raise, even at the same percentage, is calculated from a higher starting point. A 3% raise on $55,000 is $1,650, compared with $1,500 on the original $50,000. This can significantly increase long-term earnings, since the benefit of a promotion raise doesn't stop after the first year, it carries forward into every future raise calculated from that new, higher base.

## Typical promotion raise ranges by job level

While there's no universal standard, promotion raises tend to cluster into rough ranges depending on how significant the move is:

| Type of Promotion | Typical Raise Range |
|---|---|
| Lateral move with slightly more scope | 3%–7% |
| Individual contributor to team lead | 5%–10% |
| Team lead to manager | 8%–15% |
| Manager to director or senior leadership | 10%–20%+ |

These ranges are a general guide rather than a fixed rule, and actual outcomes vary widely by employer, industry, and location. A promotion that comes with a dramatic increase in scope, such as taking on an entirely new function or a much larger team, can exceed these ranges, while a promotion that's more of a title change with modest added responsibility may land toward the lower end or below it. If your promotion raise falls noticeably outside these ranges in either direction, it's worth understanding why, since it may reflect your specific company's pay structure, the broader market for your role, or simply how much the scope of your new position has actually changed.

## How to negotiate a better promotion raise

If the raise attached to a promotion feels lower than the added responsibility warrants, a few approaches can help make a stronger case.

**Research comparable roles.** Look at what similar positions pay at other companies in your industry and location. If your new title and responsibilities align with a role that commands more in the broader market, that's a concrete, well-supported reason to ask for an adjustment.

**Clarify the full scope of the new role.** Before accepting, make sure you understand exactly what's changing, not just the title. A promotion that adds significant leadership duties or a much larger budget to manage justifies a different conversation than one that's mostly a title change.

**Ask how the raise was determined.** Understanding whether your employer uses structured pay bands tied to specific job levels, or sets promotion raises case by case, can help you understand where there may be room to negotiate and where the number might be more fixed.

**Bring specific numbers, not general requests.** Rather than simply asking for "more," propose a specific percentage or salary figure based on your research into comparable roles and the added scope of the position.

**Consider timing.** If a larger raise isn't possible right now, ask whether a follow-up review in three to six months, once you've demonstrated success in the new role, could be an option. This can be a reasonable middle ground when an employer's budget is constrained at the moment of promotion itself.

## Questions to ask before accepting a promotion

Before saying yes to a promotion, it's worth looking beyond the raise percentage alone. A few questions can help you evaluate the full picture.

**Will responsibilities increase?** Understand exactly what's expected of you in the new role, not just the new title, so you can judge whether the raise matches the added workload.

**Does the new role include leadership duties?** Managing people or teams is a meaningfully different kind of work than an individual contributor role, and it's worth knowing upfront whether this is part of the promotion.

**Are bonuses changing?** Some roles come with a different bonus structure, higher or lower, which can meaningfully affect your total compensation beyond the base salary increase alone.

**Are benefits improving?** Health insurance, retirement contributions, and other benefits sometimes change with a promotion, and these can be worth a significant amount even if they don't show up directly in the salary figure.

**What are future growth opportunities?** A promotion that opens a clearer path to further advancement can be worth more over time than one that offers a bigger raise today but leads to a less defined next step.

Salary is only one part of total compensation, so weighing these questions alongside the raise percentage gives a much more complete picture of whether a promotion is actually a good deal.

## Promotion without a raise: is that normal?

Occasionally, an employee receives a new title or added responsibilities without an accompanying salary increase, sometimes described as a "dry promotion." While this does happen, it's generally not considered standard practice, and it's worth addressing directly if it happens to you.

A title change alone, without additional pay, doesn't reflect the added value you're now expected to deliver. If you find yourself in this situation, it's reasonable to ask your employer directly about the timeline for a corresponding salary adjustment, whether that's immediate or tied to a specific future milestone, such as a successful first few months in the new role. Documenting the added responsibilities clearly can also help support that conversation, whether it happens right away or down the line.

A promotion raise is a salary increase given when an employee moves into a higher-level role, reflecting the added responsibilities and expectations that come with the new position.

**How do I calculate a promotion raise percentage?**
Subtract the old salary from the new salary, divide by the old salary, and multiply by 100. For example, a raise from $48,000 to $54,000 works out to a 12.5% increase.

**Is a promotion raise different from an annual raise?**
Yes. Promotion raises are typically tied to increased responsibilities, while annual raises are often routine salary adjustments within the same role.

Promotion raises depend on factors such as industry, company policies, job level, responsibilities, and market demand, which is why there's no single standard percentage that applies everywhere.
