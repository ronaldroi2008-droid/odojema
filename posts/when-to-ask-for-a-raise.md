Asking for a raise is one of the highest-leverage conversations in a career, and one of the most avoided. Many people either wait years too long, hoping a raise will simply be offered, or ask at the wrong time with the wrong number, weakening their case before it even gets a fair hearing.

This guide covers how to tell if it's actually a good time to ask, how to figure out a realistic number, and how to build a case that's hard to say no to.

## Signs it's a good time to ask

Timing matters more than most people expect. A well-reasoned raise request made at the wrong moment can still get rejected, not because the request is unfair, but because the context works against it. Good signals include:

- **You've taken on responsibilities beyond your original role.** If your job today looks meaningfully different (and bigger) than the job you were hired for, your pay should reflect that.
- **You have measurable results to point to.** Completed projects, revenue impact, efficiency improvements, or positive feedback are all concrete evidence, far stronger than "I've been here a while."
- **It's been over a year since your last raise.** Outside of promotions, most companies review compensation annually. If a full cycle has passed with no adjustment, it's reasonable to raise the topic.
- **The company is doing well.** Raises are easier to approve out of a healthy budget. A round of layoffs or a rough earnings quarter is rarely the moment to ask, even if your performance justifies it.
- **You have a competing offer or clear market data.** Nothing clarifies a compensation conversation faster than evidence of what the market is actually paying for your role and experience level.

## Signs it might be the wrong time

- **The company just went through layoffs or a hiring freeze.** Budgets are tightest right after cuts, even if leadership insists things are stabilizing.
- **You're new to the role.** Most employers expect at least 6-12 months in a position before considering an increase, unless your responsibilities changed dramatically in that window.
- **Your recent performance has been inconsistent.** A raise request lands much better after a strong review cycle than during or right after a rocky one.
- **There's no scheduled review process and no clear budget cycle context.** Asking blind, with no sense of the company's financial position, is riskier than asking with that context in hand.

None of these make asking impossible, but they do mean the conversation may need a different approach, or might be worth delaying a few months for a stronger position.

## How much should you ask for?

This is where most raise requests go wrong: either asking for too little (leaving money on the table) or too much with no supporting evidence (undermining the request entirely).

### Start with market data

Look up your role, experience level, and location on salary comparison sites, industry surveys, or platforms like LinkedIn Salary and Glassdoor. This tells you the realistic range for your position in the current market, not what feels fair in the abstract.

### Calculate the actual dollar gap

Once you know the market range, compare it to your current pay. If market data suggests your role typically pays $65,000-$75,000 and you're currently at $58,000, that gap (roughly 12-29%) becomes your negotiating range, not a number picked out of thin air.

A [raise calculator](/raise-calculator) can help you quickly see what a given percentage increase actually translates to in dollars, both as a lump raise amount and as a new monthly take-home figure, which makes it easier to sanity-check whether your target number is realistic.

### Typical raise ranges

While every situation is different, general benchmarks can help anchor expectations:

- **Standard annual increase (cost of living / performance)**: typically 3-5%
- **Raise tied to expanded responsibilities without a title change**: often 5-10%
- **Raise tied to a promotion**: often 10-20%, sometimes more depending on the seniority jump
- **Raise justified primarily by a competing offer**: highly variable, but often needs to at least match or slightly exceed the competing offer to be effective as a retention tool

These are starting reference points, not guarantees. Your specific number should be grounded in market data and your documented impact, not a generic percentage alone.

## Building your case

A raise request is stronger when it reads like a business case, not a personal appeal. Structure it around evidence:

1. **List concrete accomplishments** from the past review period: completed projects, measurable results, positive feedback, new skills or responsibilities taken on.
2. **Tie those accomplishments to business impact** wherever possible. "Reduced processing time by 20%" or "brought in three new client accounts" is far more persuasive than a general description of effort.
3. **Bring market data** to show your current pay relative to your role and experience level.
4. **Propose a specific number**, not a vague range. A precise, well-justified ask is easier for a manager to bring to their own leadership than an open-ended "whatever you think is fair."
5. **Practice saying the number out loud** before the actual conversation. It's common for the number to feel uncomfortable to say even when it's well-justified; rehearsing removes hesitation in the moment.

## What to do if the answer is no

A rejected raise request isn't necessarily a dead end. Ask directly what would need to change, in performance, scope, or timing, for the answer to be different, and ask when it would be reasonable to revisit the conversation. This turns a "no" into a concrete plan rather than a closed door, and it signals to your manager that you're serious about the trajectory, not just making a one-time ask.

If the answer is a repeated "no" with no clear path forward, especially alongside market data showing you're meaningfully underpaid, that's often a signal worth taking seriously about whether the role, or the company, has room for your continued growth.

## Frequently asked questions

**How much of a raise is reasonable to ask for?**
It depends on the reason for the raise. Standard annual increases are typically 3-5%, expanded responsibilities often justify 5-10%, and promotions can range from 10-20% or more. The right number should be grounded in market data for your specific role, not a generic benchmark alone.

**When is the best time to ask for a raise?**
Generally after a strong performance period, when you've taken on new responsibilities, when it's been over a year since your last adjustment, and when the company is in a financially stable position. Asking right after layoffs, during a hiring freeze, or very early in a new role is typically less effective.

**Should I mention a competing offer when asking for a raise?**
It can be effective, but it should be used carefully. A genuine competing offer is strong evidence of your market value, but leading with it as a threat rather than as context can damage the relationship if the negotiation doesn't go your way.

**What if my manager says there's no budget for a raise?**
Ask what would need to change for that to be different, and when it would make sense to revisit the conversation. If there's truly no room in the current budget, getting a specific timeline for reconsideration is more useful than leaving the conversation open-ended.

**How do I know if I'm underpaid?**
Compare your current pay against market data for your role, experience level, and location using salary comparison platforms or industry surveys. A consistent gap between your pay and the market range for your position is a strong signal that a raise conversation is worth having.
