## What is an overtime pay rate?

An overtime rate is the hourly wage you're paid specifically for overtime hours, as opposed to your regular hourly wage. It's calculated by applying a multiplier to your normal hourly rate, and it's always higher than what you earn during standard hours.

The difference between your hourly wage and your overtime wage exists because overtime hours are meant to be compensated at a premium, recognizing the extra time and reduced personal availability that comes with working beyond a standard schedule. Multipliers exist to standardize that premium into a consistent, predictable rate rather than leaving it open to case-by-case negotiation.

Here's a simple example:

```
Regular Rate        = $20/hour
Overtime Multiplier = 1.5×
Overtime Rate        = $30/hour
```

That $30 figure is what you'd actually earn for each overtime hour worked, not the $20 regular rate.

## How overtime rates work

The formula for finding your overtime rate is straightforward:

```
Overtime Rate = Hourly Rate × Overtime Multiplier
```

Example:

```
Hourly Rate = $25
Multiplier  = 1.5×
Overtime Rate = $37.50
```

Once you know your overtime rate, calculating your total overtime pay for a given period just requires one more step:

```
Overtime Pay = Overtime Rate × Overtime Hours
```

This two-step structure, rate first, then pay, is useful to keep separate in your head, since confusing the two is one of the most common mistakes people make when estimating their own overtime earnings, covered in more detail later in this guide.

## Common overtime pay rates

Multipliers vary depending on the type of overtime and the employer's policy. Here's a quick reference using a $20 hourly rate as the baseline.

### Overtime rates at a glance

| Rate Type | Multiplier | $20/hr Example |
|---|---|---|
| Regular Pay | 1× | $20 |
| Light Overtime | 1.25× | $25 |
| Time and a Half | 1.5× | $30 |
| Double Time | 2× | $40 |
| Holiday Premium | 2.5× | $50 |

Not every employer uses all five of these tiers. Most rely primarily on time and a half for standard overtime, with double time and holiday premiums reserved for more specific circumstances. The sections below cover each of the main rates in more detail.

## Time and a half explained

**What is time and a half?** Time and a half means earning 1.5× your regular hourly rate for overtime hours.

```
Regular Rate          = $20
Time and a Half Rate  = $30
```

**Why it's common.** Time and a half is the standard overtime rate at many workplaces, and it's frequently the rate applied once an employee crosses a weekly hours threshold, most commonly 40 hours. It's the most widely used overtime multiplier, and if you're unsure what rate applies to your own overtime hours, 1.5× is the most likely default, though it's still worth confirming with your employer or local labor regulations.

## Double time explained

**What is double time?** Double time means earning 2× your regular hourly rate.

```
Regular Rate      = $20
Double Time Rate  = $40
```

**When double time may apply.** Double time tends to show up in more specific situations than standard time and a half, including extended overtime shifts that go well beyond a normal schedule, agreements with a specific employer that guarantee a higher rate under certain conditions, union contracts that set defined overtime terms, and certain holiday schedules where a full double rate is used rather than a smaller holiday premium.

Because double time isn't as universal as time and a half, it's worth checking your specific employment agreement or local labor law rather than assuming it applies automatically to all extended hours.

## Holiday overtime rates

**What is holiday premium pay?** Some employers pay higher overtime rates specifically for work performed on public holidays, separate from their standard overtime policy for regular extended hours.

**Example multipliers:**

| Holiday Rate | $20/hr Example |
|---|---|
| 1.5× | $30 |
| 2× | $40 |
| 2.5× | $50 |

**Important note:** Holiday overtime policies vary considerably by employer and location. Some employers simply apply their standard overtime rate to holiday hours, while others set a distinct, often higher, holiday premium. There's no universal standard here, so checking your employee handbook or asking directly is the most reliable way to know what applies to your specific situation.

## Overtime rate examples

Here are three worked examples showing how the overtime rate and overtime pay formulas connect in practice.

**Example 1: Time and a half**

```
Hourly Rate = $15
Hours       = 5

OT Rate = $15 × 1.5 = $22.50
OT Pay  = $22.50 × 5 = $112.50
```

**Example 2: Double time**

```
Hourly Rate = $25
Hours       = 8

OT Rate = $25 × 2 = $50
OT Pay  = $50 × 8 = $400
```

**Example 3: Holiday premium**

```
Hourly Rate = $20
Hours       = 6
Multiplier  = 2.5×

OT Rate = $20 × 2.5 = $50
OT Pay  = $50 × 6 = $300
```

In each example, the overtime rate is calculated first, then multiplied by the number of hours to reach the final overtime pay figure.

## How different overtime rates affect earnings

The multiplier applied to your overtime hours has a significant effect on total earnings, even when the number of hours worked stays exactly the same. Here's a direct comparison at a $20 hourly rate with 10 overtime hours:

| Hourly Rate | Multiplier | OT Hours | Overtime Pay |
|---|---|---|---|
| $20 | 1.5× | 10 | $300 |
| $20 | 2× | 10 | $400 |
| $20 | 2.5× | 10 | $500 |

**Key insight:** Higher overtime multipliers can significantly increase total earnings even when the number of overtime hours stays the same. The jump from time and a half to double time alone adds an extra $100 in this example, without a single additional hour being worked. This is why knowing exactly which multiplier applies to your hours matters just as much as knowing how many hours you actually worked.

## Which overtime rate applies to you?

There's no single overtime rate that applies universally. Which one governs your own pay depends on a few factors.

| Factor | Can Affect OT Rate? |
|---|---|
| Employer Policy | Yes |
| Employment Contract | Yes |
| Union Agreement | Yes |
| Holiday Schedule | Yes |
| Local Labor Rules | Yes |

**Employer policies.** Different employers structure their overtime differently. Some use a flat 1.5× rate for all overtime, while others apply tiered rates depending on how much overtime is worked or under what circumstances.

**Employment agreements.** Union contracts and individual employment agreements can define specific overtime rates that differ from a company's general policy, sometimes guaranteeing higher rates under defined conditions.

**Local labor rules.** Overtime regulations vary by location, including which rate applies, what threshold triggers overtime in the first place, and whether daily as well as weekly thresholds are used.

**Holiday policies.** As covered above, many employers apply a separate rate specifically for holiday work, which may or may not match their standard overtime multiplier.

Because these factors interact, the most reliable way to know your exact overtime rate is to check your employee handbook, ask your employer or HR department directly, or review the labor regulations that apply in your location.

## Common mistakes about overtime rates

A few recurring errors come up when people try to estimate their own overtime pay.

**Mistake #1: Assuming all overtime uses 1.5×.** While time and a half is the most common rate, it isn't universal. Double time, holiday premiums, and tiered multipliers all apply in specific situations, and assuming 1.5× across the board can lead to an inaccurate estimate.

**Mistake #2: Confusing overtime rate with overtime pay.** The overtime rate is your hourly wage for overtime hours (Hourly Rate × Multiplier). Overtime pay is the total amount earned (Overtime Rate × Overtime Hours). Mixing these two up is an easy way to end up with the wrong final number.

**Mistake #3: Ignoring holiday overtime rates.** If holiday work is paid at a different rate than standard overtime at your workplace, using the wrong multiplier for those specific hours will throw off your total calculation.

**Mistake #4: Using the wrong hourly rate.** This is especially common for salaried employees who need to convert their salary into an hourly figure first. Applying an overtime multiplier to an inaccurate base rate compounds the error through the rest of the calculation.

## Tracking pay when multiple rates apply

Some employees encounter more than one overtime rate within the same pay period, which can make manual tracking error-prone. A common example is someone who works standard overtime hours during the week and then picks up a holiday shift at a different premium rate.

In situations like this, it helps to separate hours into distinct buckets before applying any multiplier:

```
Regular Hours   → Hourly Rate × 1
Standard OT     → Hourly Rate × 1.5 (or applicable multiplier)
Holiday Hours   → Hourly Rate × Holiday Multiplier
```

Each bucket is calculated separately, then added together for total pay. Trying to average multiple rates into a single blended number, rather than keeping each category distinct, is a common source of errors when hours span more than one overtime tier in the same period. Keeping a simple log of which hours fall into which category, ideally as you go rather than reconstructing it later, makes this kind of multi-rate calculation far easier to check against your actual paycheck.
