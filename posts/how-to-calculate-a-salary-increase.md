# How to Calculate a Salary Increase (With Examples)

## Direct Answer

A salary increase can be calculated by multiplying your current salary by the raise percentage and adding the result to your existing pay. For example, if your salary is $50,000 and you receive a 10% raise, the increase is $5,000 and your new salary becomes $55,000.

Whether you're negotiating a raise, reviewing an offer letter, or just curious how a percentage bump translates into real dollars, the math behind a salary increase is simple once you break it into steps. This guide walks through the formula, shows worked examples at different salary levels, and covers the mistakes that trip people up most often.

Salary increases show up in a lot of different contexts, and the term itself can mean slightly different things depending on the situation. It might be an annual merit increase tied to a performance review, a cost-of-living adjustment applied company-wide, a bump that comes with a promotion, or a negotiated jump when you switch jobs entirely. In every one of these cases, the underlying math is the same: you're taking a base salary, applying a percentage (or sometimes a flat dollar amount), and arriving at a new number. What changes between scenarios isn't the formula — it's the size of the percentage and the reasoning behind it.

Understanding this calculation matters for more than just curiosity. It helps you evaluate whether an offer on the table is fair relative to your current pay, plan a household budget around an upcoming raise, and set realistic expectations before walking into a salary negotiation. It also helps you catch mistakes — both your own and, occasionally, a payroll department's — when a new paycheck doesn't match what you expected.

## Salary Increase Formula

The core formula has two parts: finding the increase, then adding it to your current pay.

**Salary Increase Amount = Current Salary × Raise Percentage**

**New Salary = Current Salary + Salary Increase Amount**

### Example

Current Salary: $50,000
Raise Percentage: 10%

Increase:
$50,000 × 0.10 = $5,000

New Salary:
$50,000 + $5,000 = $55,000

That's the entire calculation. Everything else in this guide is just applying that same logic to different numbers and situations.

## How to Find the Percentage If You Already Know Both Salaries

Sometimes you already know your old salary and your new salary — say, from an offer letter — and you want to work backward to figure out what percentage raise that actually represents. This uses a slightly different formula:

**Raise Percentage = ((New Salary − Current Salary) ÷ Current Salary) × 100**

### Example

Current Salary: $55,000
New Salary: $60,500

Difference:
$60,500 − $55,000 = $5,500

Percentage:
($5,500 ÷ $55,000) × 100 = 10%

This version of the formula is especially useful when comparing job offers, since companies don't always state the raise as a clean percentage — sometimes they just give you a new number and expect you to do the math yourself.

## How to Calculate a Salary Increase Step by Step

If you want to calculate your own raise, follow these five steps.

**Step 1: Identify your current salary.**

Use your gross annual salary — the amount before taxes and deductions. If you're paid hourly, multiply your hourly rate by your annual hours to get an annual figure first.

**Step 2: Convert the raise percentage into decimal form.**

Percentages need to be converted to decimals before you can multiply them. Divide the percentage by 100, or just move the decimal point two places to the left.

Examples:
- 5% = 0.05
- 10% = 0.10
- 15% = 0.15

**Step 3: Multiply your salary by the raise percentage.**

This gives you the dollar amount of the increase itself, not your new total salary.

**Step 4: Add the increase to your current salary.**

This is your new salary going forward.

**Step 5: Verify your new annual and monthly income.**

Divide your new annual salary by 12 to check your new monthly gross pay. This step matters because a raise that looks large annually can look surprisingly small on a single paycheck, especially after taxes.

## Salary Increase Examples

Seeing the formula applied at different salary levels makes it easier to estimate your own raise quickly.

### Example 1: 3% Raise

Current Salary: $40,000

Increase: $40,000 × 3% = $1,200

New Salary: $41,200

### Example 2: 5% Raise

Current Salary: $60,000

Increase: $60,000 × 5% = $3,000

New Salary: $63,000

### Example 3: 10% Raise

Current Salary: $75,000

Increase: $75,000 × 10% = $7,500

New Salary: $82,500

### Example 4: 20% Raise

Current Salary: $100,000

Increase: $100,000 × 20% = $20,000

New Salary: $120,000

Notice that the *percentage* stays proportional, but the *dollar amount* grows significantly as the base salary increases. A 10% raise on $40,000 is a very different number than a 10% raise on $100,000, even though the percentage is identical.

### Example 5: Hourly Rate Raise

Raises aren't always framed in annual terms. If you're paid hourly, the same formula applies directly to your rate.

Current Hourly Rate: $22.00
Raise Percentage: 6%

Increase:
$22.00 × 6% = $1.32

New Hourly Rate:
$23.32

To see the annual impact, multiply the new hourly rate by your expected annual hours (for example, 2,080 hours for a standard full-time schedule):

$23.32 × 2,080 = $48,505.60 per year

### Example 6: Negotiated Counteroffer

Sometimes a raise happens in stages during a negotiation. Say an employer initially offers 4%, and you counter for 7%. Knowing the dollar difference between the two offers can help you decide how hard to push.

Current Salary: $70,000

4% Offer:
$70,000 × 4% = $2,800 → New Salary: $72,800

7% Counter:
$70,000 × 7% = $4,900 → New Salary: $74,900

Difference Between Offers:
$74,900 − $72,800 = $2,100 per year

Framing the gap in real dollars — rather than just "4% vs. 7%" — often makes it easier to decide whether it's worth continuing to negotiate.

## What Is Considered a Good Salary Increase?

Not all raises carry the same weight, and context matters a lot when judging whether a number is generous or underwhelming. Here's a general breakdown of what different raise ranges typically represent:

**Cost-of-Living Raise: 2%–4%**
This is designed to keep your purchasing power roughly steady as prices rise, not to reward performance. It's the most common type of raise and often applied broadly across a company.

**Average Merit Raise: 3%–5%**
This reflects solid performance during a standard review cycle. It usually goes beyond cost-of-living adjustments but isn't tied to a change in role or responsibility.

**Promotion Raise: 5%–15%**
When a raise comes with a title change or expanded responsibilities, employers typically offer more to reflect the added scope of the role.

**Significant Career Move: 10%–25%+**
Switching employers, taking on a much larger role, or negotiating from a position of leverage (like a competing offer) can push raises well beyond typical internal review numbers.

Where your raise falls in this range depends on your industry, location, company performance, and how tight the labor market is for your role at the time. A 3% raise during a year of high inflation might barely keep pace with rising costs, while a 3% raise during a low-inflation year could still represent real income growth.

It also helps to think about raises relative to your tenure and role level. Early-career employees often see larger percentage jumps as they move between roles or gain in-demand skills quickly, while raises tend to flatten out somewhat in senior positions, where percentage increases translate into much larger absolute dollar amounts even at a lower rate. A 4% raise for a senior employee earning $150,000 is $6,000 — often larger in real terms than a 10% raise for someone earning $40,000.

Industry also plays a significant role. Fast-growing sectors with high competition for talent, such as certain areas of technology or specialized finance roles, tend to see larger average raises and more frequent off-cycle adjustments. More stable, slower-moving industries tend to stick closer to standard annual merit ranges.

## Compounding Raises Over Multiple Years

A single raise is easy to calculate, but raises rarely happen in isolation — most people receive some form of increase every year, and those increases compound on top of each other rather than simply adding up. This is an important distinction: a 3% raise every year for five years does **not** equal a 15% raise. It equals something slightly higher, because each year's raise is calculated on an already-larger base salary.

### Example: Compounding a 3% Raise Over 5 Years

Starting Salary: $50,000

- Year 1: $50,000 × 1.03 = $51,500
- Year 2: $51,500 × 1.03 = $53,045
- Year 3: $53,045 × 1.03 = $54,636.35
- Year 4: $54,636.35 × 1.03 = $56,275.44
- Year 5: $56,275.44 × 1.03 = $57,963.71

After five years of 3% annual raises, the salary grows to roughly $57,964 — about $2,964 more than the simple (non-compounding) estimate of $57,500 you'd get by just adding 15% in one step. The gap grows larger the more years and the higher the percentage involved. ## Percentage Raise vs. Fixed Raise

Raises are typically structured one of two ways, and it's worth understanding the difference.

### Percentage Raise

Calculated as: Current Salary × Raise %

**Benefit:** Scales with salary. Higher earners get a proportionally larger dollar increase, which keeps pay growth consistent relative to income across an organization.

### Fixed Raise

Common amounts: +$2,000, +$5,000, +$10,000

**Benefit:** Easy to understand and communicate. Everyone knows exactly what they're getting regardless of their current salary, which can simplify budgeting for both the employee and the employer.

Employers sometimes use fixed raises for standardized adjustments (like union contracts or company-wide bonuses) and percentage raises for merit-based or performance-driven increases. If you're negotiating, it's worth asking which structure is being used, since a "modest-sounding" fixed raise can actually outperform a percentage raise for lower-salaried employees, and vice versa for higher earners.

## Tips for Negotiating a Salary Increase

Knowing how to calculate a raise is only half the picture — knowing how to ask for one, and evaluate what you're offered, is the other half. A few practical tips:

**Come with a target number, not just a target percentage.** Employers respond better to a specific dollar figure backed by research (market rate data, comparable offers, or documented achievements) than to a vague request for "more."

**Benchmark against market rate, not just your last raise.** If your last raise was 3%, that doesn't mean 3% is fair this time. Compare your salary to current market rates for your role, location, and experience level using a salary calculator, since market conditions shift year to year independent of your personal raise history.

**Separate cost-of-living from merit.** If inflation was 4% last year and you only received a 3% raise, you effectively took a pay cut in real terms — even though the number on paper went up. It's reasonable to ask that cost-of-living adjustments be treated separately from performance-based increases.

**Time your ask around performance reviews or after a clear win.** Raises tied to a specific accomplishment, a completed project, or a formal review cycle tend to land better than requests that come with no clear trigger.

**Get the final number in writing.** Verbal agreements about raise percentages sometimes don't translate cleanly into payroll changes. Confirm the new salary, effective date, and any prorated amounts in writing before treating the raise as final.

## Common Salary Increase Mistakes

A few recurring errors can throw off your raise calculations or your expectations around them.

**Mistake 1: Confusing percentage points with percentages.**

Going from a 3% raise to a 5% raise is a 2 percentage point increase, but it's *not* a 2% increase in your raise. In relative terms, it's actually a much larger jump (about 67% more raise than before). Mixing these two concepts up is one of the most common sources of confusion in salary discussions.

**Mistake 2: Calculating monthly raises from annual salary incorrectly.**

Some people mistakenly apply the raise percentage to their monthly salary and then multiply by 12, which usually still works out fine — but errors creep in when people forget to convert monthly figures to annual ones first, or apply the percentage twice by accident. The cleanest approach is to always calculate the raise on your annual salary, then divide the new total by 12 to check the monthly amount.

**Mistake 3: Ignoring taxes.**

A raise increases your gross pay, not your take-home pay one-for-one. Depending on your tax bracket, deductions, and benefits elections, your net increase per paycheck will be smaller than the raw percentage suggests. It's worth running your new salary through a paycheck calculator if you want an accurate take-home estimate.

**Mistake 4: Not considering inflation.**

A 3% raise sounds like real growth, but if inflation for that year is also around 3%, your purchasing power has effectively stayed flat. Comparing your raise percentage to the current inflation rate gives a more honest picture of whether your income is actually growing in real terms. If you're curious how raises stack up over several years rather than just one cycle, a [raise compounding calculator](https://odojema.com/raise-compounding-calculator) can show how repeated annual increases build on each other over time.

**Mistake 5: Forgetting that mid-year raises are often prorated.**

If a raise takes effect partway through the year rather than at the start, your total annual earnings for that year won't reflect a full year at the new salary. For example, a 10% raise that starts in July only affects about half the year's pay, so your actual year-end earnings will land somewhere between the old and new salary — not at the new salary outright. This is a common source of confusion when comparing "raise percentage" to what actually shows up on a W-2 or year-end pay summary.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How do you calculate a salary increase?**

Multiply your current salary by the raise percentage, then add the result to your existing salary.

**How much is a 5% raise on a $50,000 salary?**

A 5% raise on a $50,000 salary equals $2,500, resulting in a new salary of $52,500.

**How much is a 10% raise on a $60,000 salary?**

A 10% raise equals $6,000, increasing the salary to $66,000.

**Is a 3% salary increase good?**

A 3% raise is commonly considered a standard annual merit or cost-of-living adjustment. It's neither exceptional nor disappointing on its own — its value depends largely on the current inflation rate and industry norms.

**What is the formula for calculating a raise?**

New Salary = Current Salary + (Current Salary × Raise Percentage)

**How do I calculate what percentage raise I received?**

Subtract your old salary from your new salary, divide that difference by your old salary, then multiply by 100. For example, going from $55,000 to $60,500 is a 10% raise: (($60,500 − $55,000) ÷ $55,000) × 100 = 10%.

**Do raises compound each year?**

Yes. Each year's raise is calculated on top of the previous year's new (already-increased) salary, not the original starting salary. Over several years, this means your total pay growth will be slightly higher than simply adding up each year's percentage.
