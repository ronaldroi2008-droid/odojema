A 10% raise increases your current salary by 10%. To calculate it, multiply your current salary by 0.10 and add the result to your existing pay. For example, a $50,000 salary with a 10% raise becomes $55,000, adding $5,000 per year before taxes.

This guide shows the formulas, worked examples at different salaries, what a 10% raise adds per month and per paycheck, and how to tell whether 10% is a good raise for your situation. You can also check any number instantly with our [raise calculator](https://odojema.com/raise-calculator).

## How to calculate a 10% raise

There are two ways to get the answer, and both give the same result. The first finds the raise amount:

```
Raise Amount = Current Salary × 10%
Raise Amount = Current Salary × 0.10
```

The second finds your new salary in one step:

```
New Salary = Current Salary × 1.10
```

Here is the calculation for a $50,000 salary using both methods:

```
Method 1: $50,000 × 0.10 = $5,000 raise
          $50,000 + $5,000 = $55,000 new salary

Method 2: $50,000 × 1.10 = $55,000 new salary
```

The second method is faster because you skip a step. Multiplying by 1.10 means "100% of what I earn now, plus 10% more."

**How do you work backward from the numbers?** If you already know your old and new salary and want to check the percentage, use this formula:

```
Raise Percentage = ((New Salary − Old Salary) ÷ Old Salary) × 100
((55,000 − 50,000) ÷ 50,000) × 100 = 10%
```

Every figure here is gross, meaning before taxes and deductions. Your take-home increase will be smaller. To skip the manual math, enter your salary into the [raise calculator](https://odojema.com/raise-calculator) and it returns your raise amount and new salary right away.

## How much more per month?

Divide the annual raise by 12. Using a $50,000 salary:

```
Raise Amount     = $5,000
Monthly Increase = $5,000 ÷ 12 = $416.67 per month
```

That is about $417 of extra gross income each month. Your new monthly gross pay goes from $4,166.67 to $4,583.33. For a full breakdown of your income by week, month, and year, try the [salary calculator](https://odojema.com/salary-calculator).

## How much more per paycheck?

The amount that lands in each paycheck depends on your pay schedule. For a $5,000 annual raise:

```
Weekly:       $5,000 ÷ 52 = $96.15 per week
Biweekly:     $5,000 ÷ 26 = $192.31 per pay period
Semi-monthly: $5,000 ÷ 24 = $208.33 per pay period
Monthly:      $5,000 ÷ 12 = $416.67 per pay period
```

These are all before taxes.

**What will you actually keep after taxes?** The exact number depends on your filing status, your state, and your deductions. Extra income is generally taxed at your marginal rate, and payroll taxes such as Social Security and Medicare also apply. As a rough ballpark, many workers keep somewhere around 70% to 85% of a raise, which would turn a $5,000 raise into about $3,500 to $4,250 a year. Your paystub after the raise takes effect is the most reliable way to see the real figure, and the [salary calculator](https://odojema.com/salary-calculator) can help you estimate it in advance.

## Is a 10% raise good?

Yes. A 10% raise is generally considered a significant salary increase. Many annual merit raises fall in a range of roughly 2% to 5%, though this varies by year, industry, and company size. Compared with that range, 10% is about double a typical yearly adjustment.

A raise of 10% is often associated with situations like these:

- **Promotion:** Moving into a role with more responsibility or a higher pay grade.
- **Exceptional performance:** Recognition for results well above expectations.
- **Market adjustment:** Bringing your pay in line with what the market pays for your role.
- **Retention incentive:** An employer's effort to keep you when you have other options.

Salary surveys and typical raise budgets change from year to year, so check a current source for your field and location before you use any average as a benchmark.

Keep in mind that "good" also depends on context. A 10% raise on a salary that was well below market may simply bring you to fair pay, while 10% on an already competitive salary is a strong reward. Look at both the percentage and where the new number lands compared with similar jobs. ## 10% raise vs promotion

Standard annual raises and promotion raises usually fall in different ranges:

```
Standard raise:  3% to 5%
Promotion raise: 5% to 15% or more
```

Promotions often come with larger salary increases because the job itself changes. You are usually taking on a bigger scope, more decision-making, more people to manage, or more specialized work. Employers price the new role, not just your performance in the old one, and many companies attach a new pay band to the new title.

That means a 10% raise can mean different things depending on how you got it:

- **A 10% merit raise with no change in role** is unusually generous and suggests strong performance.
- **A 10% raise that comes with a promotion** is within the normal range, and may even be on the low end if your responsibilities have grown a lot.

If you are offered a promotion with a raise below 5%, it is reasonable to ask how the pay was set and whether it matches the new role in the market.

## 10% raise vs changing jobs

An internal raise is not the only way to increase your pay. Here is how the main options compare:

- **Internal raise:** Usually the smallest increase, often in the 2% to 5% range, but it comes with stability, no ramp-up period, and a known workplace.
- **Promotion:** Often larger, commonly 5% to 15% or more, and it can build your title and experience along with your income.
- **External job offer:** Can bring a bigger jump, especially if your current pay has fallen behind the market. Because a new employer prices you against current hiring rates, external offers can exceed what an internal raise budget allows.
- **Career growth:** A move that raises your pay less today but puts you on a faster path to promotion can be worth more over several years.

Changing employers may lead to a larger increase in situations like these:

- Your pay is noticeably below what job postings for your role offer.
- You have been at the same company for several years with only small annual raises.
- Your employer says a promotion or market adjustment is not possible.
- Your skills have grown, but your title and pay have not.

Changing jobs also has risks. You may lose seniority, benefits you have built up, or job security. Before deciding, compare full offers, including benefits, bonuses, and PTO, using the [job offer comparison calculator](https://odojema.com/job-offer-comparison-calculator). A higher salary does not always mean higher total compensation.

## How inflation affects a 10% raise

A raise only improves your finances if it is larger than the rise in prices. The gap between your raise and inflation is your real gain in purchasing power. A quick estimate is to subtract the inflation rate from your raise percentage.

**Scenario A: Inflation is 3%**

```
Raise         = 10%
Inflation     = 3%
Real Gain     = about 7%
```

On a $50,000 salary, prices rising 3% would mean you need about $51,500 to keep the same standard of living. Your new salary of $55,000 leaves you about $3,500 ahead in real terms.

**Scenario B: Inflation is 8%**

```
Raise         = 10%
Inflation     = 8%
Real Gain     = about 2%
```

At 8% inflation, you would need about $54,000 to maintain your lifestyle. Your new salary of $55,000 leaves you only about $1,000 ahead. The raise looks large, but most of it is absorbed by higher prices.

The subtraction shortcut is slightly off because the two percentages compound. The more precise formula is to divide: 1.10 ÷ 1.03 is about 1.068, or roughly a 6.8% real gain in Scenario A, and 1.10 ÷ 1.08 is about 1.019, or about 1.9% in Scenario B. The shortcut is close enough for a quick check.

**Why purchasing power matters.** Two people can both get a 10% raise and end up in very different positions depending on what is happening to the cost of living where they live. If inflation is lower than your raise, your purchasing power increases. If inflation matches or exceeds your raise, you are not really getting ahead, and a 3% raise in a low-inflation year can be worth more than a 10% raise in a high-inflation year. ## Common mistakes when calculating raises

A few errors come up again and again when people work out what a raise is worth.

**Mistake 1: Calculating from net pay instead of gross salary.** Raises are set as a percentage of gross salary, not take-home pay. If you apply 10% to your net pay, you will get a number that is too low, and it will not match what your employer offers.

**Mistake 2: Ignoring taxes.** A $5,000 raise does not add $5,000 to your bank account. Income tax and payroll taxes reduce the amount you keep, and a larger raise may be taxed at a higher marginal rate. Plan around your after-tax increase.

**Mistake 3: Confusing raise amount with raise percentage.** A $5,000 raise is 10% on a $50,000 salary but only 5% on a $100,000 salary. Always check which one you are being told, and use the [raise calculator](https://odojema.com/raise-calculator) to convert between the two. The same dollar amount can be generous or modest depending on your starting pay.

**Mistake 4: Ignoring inflation.** A raise that is smaller than inflation is effectively a pay cut. Always compare your raise percentage with the current rate of price increases.

Two more worth mentioning: forgetting that a raise may change your bonus or retirement contribution if those are calculated as a percentage of salary, and forgetting to account for the timing, since some raises take effect mid-year and add less to your first year's earnings than the full annual figure.
